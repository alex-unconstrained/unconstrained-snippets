Landscape of Great Schools: working framework

Draft 0.1 · UNNAMED · for internal review · compiled 24 September 2026
Prepared for UnconstrainED. First intended use: the "Look outward" phase of the International School of Curitiba strategic direction proposal, and future engagements.

0. About this draft

What it is. A first attempt at a reference framework describing where leading schools, universities and learning organisations are right now, 2023 to September 2026. It is meant to be something we can bring into a room with a Board: organised, evidence-rated, and honest about what is proven and what is hype.

How it was made. Five AI research agents (Claude, run by UnconstrainED on 24 September 2026) each searched the web in one area:

  1. K-12 schools worldwide
  2. Higher education
  3. Professional and workplace learning
  4. Cross-cutting evidence and existing frameworks
  5. International schools, with Brazil and Latin America

Each agent was told to name the institution, give a source URL and date for every claim, and rate the evidence. Their reports were then combined into this document. Every example below keeps its source. The research was done with limited search budgets. Some primary sites blocked automated access, so a few claims rest on secondary coverage; these are marked.

Before anything here reaches a client: a person should check each claim used against its source. See section 8 for the items the agents themselves flagged as unverified or conflicting.

Evidence ratings used throughout

  • Strong: randomised controlled trials (RCTs), meta-analyses, large administrative data, or official primary documents.
  • Emerging: single or small trials, preprints, quasi-experiments, large self-report surveys, or pilots.
  • Anecdotal: announcements, institutional self-reports, press and marketing.

1. The headline: six things the evidence says

  1. Design beats access. AI can produce large learning gains or real learning losses, depending on how its use is designed. AI that provides the scaffold helps: hints, Socratic questions, teacher mediation, coaching human tutors. AI that provides the effort by giving answers or writing the text raises practice scores and lowers real learning. This is the single strongest finding of 2024–26, and it holds in K-12, higher education and the workplace.
  2. Everyone is already using it. Student and staff use is near universal: 94% of UK undergraduates, 54% of US teens for schoolwork, 84% of Brazilian students. So the strategic question is no longer whether to adopt AI but how its use is designed and governed.
  3. Assessment is where the pressure lands first. Unsupervised take-home work no longer proves what a student can do alone. Leading institutions now split assessment into a secure lane and an AI-open lane, and they are bringing back oral defence.
  4. The entry-level rung is changing. Hiring of young workers in AI-exposed jobs has fallen. Entry-level roles now ask for "senior" capacities earlier: judgement, client work, owning outcomes. Human skills and judgement about AI are becoming the core of employability, not extras.
  5. Organisation beats individual. In schools and workplaces alike, culture, leadership modelling, policy and teacher preparation explain more of AI's impact than individual skill or the tool itself.
  6. Relationships are the counterweight. Leading schools are investing deliberately in belonging, oracy (confident spoken language) and adult-student relationships, while phone restrictions and AI companions reshape attention and connection.

2. The framework: nine dimensions in three clusters

Most existing frameworks list learner competencies (OECD, UNESCO, the EC–OECD AI Literacy framework, ISTE, CASEL) or set out principles and risk rules (national guidance). None gives a school-level, observable, evidence-rated picture of leading practice that spans K-12, higher education and professional learning. That gap is our opportunity.

What makes this framework distinctive:

  • It describes what you would see on an ordinary Tuesday, not aspirations.
  • Every practice carries an evidence rating.
  • Each dimension has stages and anti-patterns.
  • It uses the same dimensions across sectors.
  • It maps to external frameworks and regulation, so compliance comes built in.

The nine dimensions:

  • Cluster A, Learning: 1 Learning design for cognitive ownership · 2 Assessment you can trust · 3 A graduate profile made real · 4 Real-world learning and pathways
  • Cluster B, People: 5 Teacher expertise and agency · 6 The human core · 7 AI fluency: critical, creative, civic
  • Cluster C, System: 8 Governance, safety and data stewardship · 9 Strategy and evidence culture

Stages used for each dimension: Starting → Developing → Leading.

A1. Learning design for cognitive ownership

The shift. Leading institutions design AI use so that students still do the thinking. The AI provides hints, questions and explanations rather than answers and finished text. They check what students can do without AI, and they use AI to deliver more of the practices already proven to work (feedback, retrieval practice, tutoring, metacognition), not to replace them.

Stages

  • Starting: open chatbot access with no design.
  • Developing: task-level AI rules; tutor-mode tools in pilots.
  • Leading: hint-first tools chosen for how they behave as teachers; students taught to use AI for explanation, not automation; unaided performance checked regularly; AI deployed to extend feedback and tutoring.

Anti-patterns

  • Buying licences and calling it a strategy.
  • Judging success by practice scores or engagement.
  • AI-first models with no independent evaluation.

Evidence

  • Bastani et al., high-school maths, Turkey (PNAS, June 2025; Strong). Unguarded GPT-4 raised practice performance by 48%. Once access was removed, students did worse on the exam than those who never had it; the figure is reported as −17% or about −0.19 SD, see section 8. A hint-only "GPT Tutor" version avoided most of the harm. https://www.pnas.org/doi/10.1073/pnas.2422633122
  • Harvard physics AI tutor (Kestin et al., Scientific Reports, June 2025; Strong but single-site). A purpose-built tutor produced median learning gains more than double those of an in-class active-learning session. https://www.nature.com/articles/s41598-025-97652-6
  • World Bank, Edo State, Nigeria (Policy Research Working Paper 11125, May 2025; Emerging–Strong). Six weeks of teacher-supervised, paired GPT-4 tutoring gave about 0.3 SD, described as roughly 1.5–2 years of typical learning. https://openknowledge.worldbank.org/entities/publication/15e1ff08-15ae-4f7a-b2a8-d146e6c113ee
  • Stanford Tutor CoPilot (Oct 2024; Strong). AI coaching human tutors in real time: +4 percentage points in mastery, +9 for students of the weakest tutors, about US$20 per tutor per year. https://edworkingpapers.com/ai24-1054
  • Contractor & Reyes (arXiv, July 2026; Emerging, preprint). Using AI to explain ("augmentation") kept learning gains; using AI to write ("automation") lost them once AI was removed. https://arxiv.org/abs/2607.08849
  • Anthropic coding-skills RCT (Jan 2026; Emerging). Junior engineers learning with AI scored 17% lower on comprehension, unless they used AI to ask for explanations. https://www.anthropic.com/research/AI-assistance-coding-skills
  • The durable core AI should amplify (Strong)
    • Tutoring: about 0.37 SD pooled; effects shrink at scale, from about 0.44 to about 0.16 SD (Nickow et al. 2020, https://www.nber.org/papers/w27476; Kraft et al. 2024, https://edworkingpapers.com/ai24-1031).
    • Feedback: +6 months; metacognition: +7 to +8 months (EEF Teaching and Learning Toolkit, https://educationendowmentfoundation.org.uk/education-evidence/teaching-learning-toolkit).
    • Retrieval practice: g = 0.61 (Adesope et al. 2017, https://journals.sagepub.com/doi/abs/10.3102/0034654316689306).
  • Caution on AI-first private models (Anecdotal). Alpha School's claims have not been independently verified (Scientific American, 28 Aug 2026, https://www.scientificamerican.com/article/alpha-schools-ai-teaching-model-is-expanding-does-it-work/).

Across sectors

  • Higher ed: Anthropic's "learning mode" and Georgia Tech's Jill Watson, a course-grounded virtual assistant that was correct 78.7% of the time vs 30.7% for a generic assistant (https://news.research.gatech.edu/2025/09/02/georgia-techs-jill-watson-outperforms-chatgpt-real-classrooms).
  • Professional: the "jagged frontier". Harvard/BCG consultants with AI were more than 40% better on tasks inside AI's capability and worse on a task outside it (https://papers.ssrn.com/sol3/papers.cfm?abstract_id=4573321).

Board question: For every AI tool our students use, what stops it doing the thinking for them, and how would we know if it were?

A2. Assessment you can trust

The shift. The move is from detecting AI to assuring learning. Leading institutions run a secure lane (supervised, oral or observed) and an open lane (AI allowed and designed for learning). They plan assurance across a whole programme, not task by task, and ask students to document their process. They have stopped relying on AI detectors.

Stages

  • Starting: bans plus detectors.
  • Developing: declared AI-use levels per task (e.g. the AI Assessment Scale).
  • Leading: programme-level assurance; regular oral defence; process portfolios; no penalty based on a detector alone.

Anti-patterns

  • Sanctions based on AI-detector scores.
  • Take-home essays still counted as proof of what a student can do alone.
  • Different rules in every department.

Evidence and exemplars

  • University of Sydney "two-lane" approach (Nov 2024; Strong as policy). https://www.sydney.edu.au/news-opinion/news/2024/11/27/university-of-sydney-ai-assessment-policy.html
  • TEQSA, Australia's national regulator (Sep 2025). Programme-level, unit-level or hybrid pathways to assuring learning; it states that detection cannot guarantee integrity. https://www.teqsa.gov.au/sites/default/files/2025-09/enacting-assessment-reform-in-a-time-of-artificial-intelligence.pdf
  • Oral exams returning at Cornell, Penn, NYU and UC San Diego (Fortune, 25 Mar 2026; Emerging). https://fortune.com/2026/03/25/oral-exams-colleges-anti-ai-teaching-method-gen-z-stare/
  • Curtin University switched off AI-writing detection from 1 January 2026. https://www.curtin.edu.au/news/oasis-news/update-on-turnitin-ai-detection-tool/
  • AI Assessment Scale (Perkins, Roe & Furze, 2025). Five levels, from "No AI" to "AI exploration". https://leonfurze.com/wp-content/uploads/2025/09/JUTLPFinalPerkins_JUTLP_2025.pdf
  • International Baccalaureate
    • It does not ban AI but requires it to be cited. The test is whether a student used AI to learn or to pretend.
    • From the May 2027 assessment, the Extended Essay must be authenticated by both student and supervisor.
    • Digital DP exams begin in May 2026.
    • Sources: https://ibo.org/programmes/artificial-intelligence-ai-in-learning-teaching-and-assessment/ (verified via https://libguides.isb.cn/teachai); https://www.aacrao.org/edge/emergent-news/ib-launches-online-ibdp-exams-in-2026/
  • Scale of use (Strong). HEPI 2026: 94% of UK undergraduates use generative AI for assessed work, and 12% paste AI text directly. https://www.hepi.ac.uk/reports/student-generative-ai-survey-2026/

Board question: Which of our assessments still show what a student can do alone, and which have we simply not re-examined?

A3. A graduate profile made real

The shift. Many schools have a "portrait of a graduate". The leaders go further: they redesign assessment, credit and transcripts around it, so the competencies appear on a student's record and universities and employers believe them.

Stages

  • Starting: a portrait on a poster.
  • Developing: competencies built into units and reports.
  • Leading: competencies assessed with credible tools and recorded alongside grades.

Anti-pattern: a portrait that never changes assessment.

Evidence and exemplars

  • New York State adopted a Portrait of a Graduate (July 2025). Regents exams stop being a graduation requirement from fall 2027. https://www.chalkbeat.org/newyork/2025/07/14/new-york-board-of-regents-exam-portrait-graduate-requirements/
  • CIS × Melbourne Metrics "New Metrics for International Schools" (Nov 2024; Emerging). Seven competencies are assessed through teacher judgement; 23 schools in 13 countries are piloting it. Western Academy of Beijing joined in Oct 2025.
    • https://www.cois.org/about-cis/news/post/~board/news/post/developing-new-metrics-for-learner-success-a-new-program-in-partnership-with-melbourne-metrics
    • https://www.wab-edu.cn/whats-happening/learning-news/view-page/~board/news/post/melbourne-metrics
  • Mastery Transcript Consortium, acquired by ETS in May 2024 (Emerging; member numbers are self-reported). https://www.ets.org/news/press-releases/ets-mastery-transcript-consortium-mtc-join-forces-scale-skills-transcript.html
  • Admissions counter-signal (Strong as policy). As essays become unreliable, selective universities have turned to signals that are harder to fake.
    • MIT, Dartmouth, Yale, Brown and Harvard reinstated SAT/ACT requirements in 2024.
    • UCAS replaced the free-form personal statement with three structured questions for 2026 entry.
    • Sources: https://www.thecrimson.com/article/2024/4/11/harvard-sat-act-admissions-requirement/; https://www.ucas.com/applying/applying-to-university/writing-your-personal-statement/how-to-write-your-personal-statement-for-2026-entry-onwards
  • Professional parallel
    • Employers increasingly want evidence of skills. Canva and Zapier assess candidates' AI fluency directly (https://www.theregister.com/2025/06/11/canva_coding_assistant_job_interviews/; https://zapier.com/blog/raising-ai-fluency-bar-in-hiring/).
    • But skills-based hiring still trails the rhetoric: fewer than 1 in 700 hires benefited in 2023 (HBS/Burning Glass, https://www.hbs.edu/bigs/joseph-fuller-college-degree-gap).

Board question: If our graduate profile is real, where does it show up on a student's record, and would a university or employer believe it?

A4. Real-world learning and pathways

The shift. As AI absorbs routine cognitive work and entry-level jobs change, leading institutions expand authentic work: projects with real audiences, internships, partners and applied problem-solving. They build judgement, client-facing skills and ownership of outcomes earlier.

Stages

  • Starting: occasional projects.
  • Developing: structured PBL units.
  • Leading: a meaningful share of every student's week is work that matters to someone outside the school, assessed rigorously.

Anti-pattern: "activity without learning": weak PBL with no rigour.

Evidence and exemplars

  • Knowledge in Action (randomised trial; Strong). Project-based AP courses raised pass rates by about 8–10 points. https://www.lucasedresearch.org/research/knowledge-in-action/
  • Big Picture Learning (Emerging). Internship-based schooling; 74% of working alumni found jobs through internship contacts. https://education-reimagined.org/findings-from-the-big-picture-learning-longitudinal-study/
  • Labour-market signals
    • Stanford "Canaries" (Aug 2026; Strong, descriptive): employment of 22–25-year-olds in highly AI-exposed jobs is about 19% below that of less-exposed peers, mainly through fewer hires. https://digitaleconomy.stanford.edu/news/canariesaug26/
    • PwC 2026: AI-exposed entry-level roles are seven times more likely to require senior-level skills. https://www.pwc.com/gx/en/news-room/press-releases/2026/pwc-2026-ai-jobs-barometer.html
    • IBM, by contrast, is tripling entry-level hiring by redesigning junior roles around customers and judgement. https://www.axios.com/2026/02/13/ai-ibm-tech-jobs
  • WEF Future of Jobs 2025. 39% of core skills will change by 2030. Analytical thinking tops the list, alongside AI and big data, resilience, creativity and curiosity. https://www.weforum.org/publications/the-future-of-jobs-report-2025/
  • Lifelong learning systems. The UK Lifelong Learning Entitlement (from Jan 2027) and Singapore's SkillsFuture Level-Up. The "one degree for life" story is weakening. https://www.gov.uk/apply-for-student-finance-lifelong-learning-entitlement; https://www.moe.gov.sg/news/press-releases/20240304-new-skillsfuture-level-up-programme-to-boost-mid-career-upskilling-and-reskilling

Board question: What share of a student's week is work that matters to someone outside the school?

B5. Teacher expertise and agency

The shift. Leading systems train and equip teachers before students, pair tools with clear policy, and treat AI as a coach and a way to save time. They decide deliberately what the saved time is for. Teachers keep authority over judgements: AI never decides grades or discipline.

Stages

  • Starting: individual teachers experimenting alone.
  • Developing: institutional tools plus training.
  • Leading: teachers first; peer-champion networks; an agreed policy on AI in grading and on reuse of teachers' materials; saved time reinvested in feedback and relationships.

Anti-patterns

  • Rushed top-down rollouts. South Korea's AI textbooks were stripped of textbook status within months.
  • Using teachers' material without their consent (ASU's "Atom").

Evidence and exemplars

  • EEF/NFER trial, England (Dec 2024; Strong). ChatGPT plus a guide cut lesson-planning time by 31%, with no loss of quality. https://educationendowmentfoundation.org.uk/projects-and-evaluation/projects/choices-in-edtech-using-generative-ai-chatgpt-for-ks3-science-lesson-preparation-2024-teacher-choices-trial
  • Gallup/Walton
    • Weekly AI users save about 5.9 hours a week (June 2025). https://news.gallup.com/poll/691967/three-teachers-weekly-saving-six-weeks-year.aspx
    • Only 18% of US teachers have formal AI guidance (May 2026). https://news.gallup.com/poll/710534/teachers-receive-no-formal-guidance.aspx
  • Estonia "AI Leap". Teachers trained and equipped before students; the student tool is designed to guide thinking. https://tihupe.ee/en/
  • Brazil's Ministry of Education (MEC). Nine free AI courses for teachers, in Portuguese (Aug 2026). https://www.gov.br/mec/pt-br/assuntos/noticias/2026/agosto/mec-oferece-nove-cursos-gratuitos-de-ia-para-professores
  • Colégio Bandeirantes, São Paulo. A good-practice AI manual co-written with faculty (Feb 2025). https://colband.net.br/digiband/conheca-o-manual-de-boas-praticas-para-o-uso-da-ia/
  • Organisation beats individual (Emerging). Microsoft's 2026 Work Trend Index found organisational factors explain more than twice the reported AI impact of individual factors. https://www.microsoft.com/en-us/worklab/work-trend-index/agents-human-agency-and-the-opportunity-for-every-organization
  • Team staffing (Emerging–Strong). Opportunity Culture's multi-classroom teams gave students close to half a year of extra learning. https://www.opportunityculture.org/2026/03/24/new-research-2-3x-schoolwide-high-growth-learning-nationally-high-growth-by-teaching-teams-with-opportunity-culture-staffing-design/

Board question: If AI gives each teacher back several hours a week, what have we decided they will do with it?

B6. The human core: belonging, relationships, wellbeing, oracy

The shift. As AI gets cheaper and is everywhere, the value of school shifts toward what only people can do. That means being known well by adults, belonging, spoken communication, collaboration and ethical judgement. Phone restrictions have become mainstream, and AI companions are the new wellbeing frontier.

Stages

  • Starting: a phone policy as the wellbeing plan.
  • Developing: advisory and SEL programmes.
  • Leading: every student known well by at least one adult; oracy built into lessons; belonging measured; explicit guidance on AI companions and on using AI for emotional support.

Anti-pattern: bans used in place of positive relational design.

Evidence and exemplars

  • Belonging (Walton et al., Science, 2023; Strong, context-dependent). A short belonging exercise raised first-year completion across 22 institutions, but only where the context offered real opportunities to belong. https://www.science.org/doi/10.1126/science.ade4420
  • Social and emotional learning (Cipriano et al., 2023; Strong). A meta-analysis of 424 studies found consistent positive effects.
  • Phone restrictions (Emerging and mixed)
    • The University of Birmingham SMART Schools study found restrictive policies alone made no difference to wellbeing or attainment. https://www.birmingham.ac.uk/news/2025/school-bans-alone-not-enough-to-tackle-negative-impacts-of-phone-and-social-media-use
    • Dutch schools report better focus after the national ban (self-report). https://www.japantimes.co.jp/news/2025/07/05/world/dutch-survey-phone-ban-focus/
    • Brazil's Law 15.100 (Jan 2025) now restricts phones in all schools.
  • Oracy Education Commission, England (Oct 2024). Makes the case for oracy as a "fourth R" alongside reading, writing and arithmetic, partly because of AI. https://oracyeducationcommission.co.uk/oec-report/
  • AI companions. 72% of US teens have tried AI companions (Common Sense Media, July 2025), and about 15% of UK undergraduates use AI for companionship (HEPI 2026). https://www.commonsensemedia.org/research/talk-trust-and-trade-offs-how-and-why-teens-use-ai-companions
  • Human skills premium (Strong long-run). Deming finds social skills earn rising wage returns. Klarna reversed its AI-only customer service, citing "lower quality". https://www.nber.org/digest/nov15/growing-importance-social-skills-labor-market; https://www.forbes.com/sites/quickerbettertech/2025/05/18/business-tech-news-klarna-reverses-on-ai-says-customers-like-talking-to-people/
  • Practice tool: relationship mapping (Harvard's Making Caring Common). https://mcc.gse.harvard.edu/whats-new/seen-and-heard-the-power-of-caring-adults

Board question: Now that phones are settled by law, what is our positive plan for connection, conversation and belonging, and how do we measure it?

B7. AI fluency: critical, creative, civic

The shift. AI literacy is becoming compulsory from the early years (UAE, China, Estonia, Brazil's Piauí state) and will be measured internationally by PISA 2029. Leading institutions go beyond tool training. They build:

  • understanding of how AI works and fails;
  • "frontier judgement": when to use AI, when not to, and how to check it;
  • an original voice that resists homogenisation;
  • awareness of AI's social and environmental costs.

Stages

  • Starting: prompt tips.
  • Developing: AI literacy lessons.
  • Leading: discipline-specific AI judgement taught and assessed, age by age, mapped to AILit/PISA and national standards.

Anti-patterns

  • "Fluency" defined as tool use only.
  • No age differentiation.

Evidence and exemplars

  • EC–OECD AI Literacy Framework (AILit). Four domains: Engage, Create, Manage and Shape AI. It underpins PISA 2029's Media & AI Literacy assessment. https://ailiteracyframework.org/
  • UNESCO AI competency frameworks for students and teachers (Sept 2024). https://www.unesco.org/en/articles/what-you-need-know-about-unescos-new-ai-competency-frameworks-students-and-teachers
  • UWCSEA, Singapore. Published age-by-age technology and AI rules. Student use of generative AI is "extremely limited until at least the end of Middle School", with AI literacy taught inside a digital literacy curriculum. https://www.uwcsea.edu.sg/learning/technology-at-uwcsea
  • Ohio State University. Every graduate from the Class of 2029 is to be "AI fluent" in their own discipline. https://news.osu.edu/ohio-state-launches-bold-ai-fluency-initiative-to-redefine-learning-and-innovation/
  • Homogenisation risk (Doshi & Hauser, Science Advances, 2024; Emerging). AI-assisted stories were rated more creative but became more similar to each other. https://www.science.org/doi/10.1126/sciadv.adn5290
  • Deskilling signals (Emerging). After routine AI use, endoscopists' detection rate without AI fell from 28.4% to 22.4% (Lancet Gastroenterology & Hepatology, 2025). Knowledge workers who trust AI more think less critically (Microsoft Research/CMU, 2025). https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/40816301/; https://dl.acm.org/doi/full/10.1145/3706598.3713778

Board question: By 2027, can we show what our students understand about how AI works and where it fails, not just which tools they can use?

C8. Governance, safety and data stewardship

The shift. Governance of AI is moving from IT policy to strategy. The main elements:

  • risk-tiered inventories of tools;
  • vendor standards;
  • age-appropriate access;
  • transparency with families;
  • safeguarding leads;
  • published policies.

In Brazil much of this is now law.

Stages

  • Starting: an acceptable-use policy.
  • Developing: an approved tool list and data agreements.
  • Leading: a risk-tiered inventory; one integrated policy meeting every regulator; public, revisable guidance; regular review.

Anti-patterns

  • Shadow AI.
  • Emotion recognition or social scoring.
  • Automated grading or progression decisions.

Evidence and exemplars

  • Brazil: National Education Council (CNE) AI guidelines (approved 1 Sep 2026; awaiting ratification by the Ministry of Education; 12 months to comply)
    • Risk tiers.
    • No automated decisions on grades, promotion or discipline.
    • No automated essay marking.
    • No punishment based on AI detectors alone.
    • No emotion recognition or social scoring.
    • No unsupervised student AI use in early childhood and the early primary years.
    • Sources: https://porvir.org/cne-diretrizes-inteligencia-artificial-escola-universidade/; https://revistaeducacao.com.br/2026/09/04/cne-aprovou-diretrizes-de-ia-na-educacao/
  • Brazil: MEC reference framework for responsible AI in education (early 2026). https://www.gov.br/mec/pt-br/referencial-de-ia-na-educacao
  • Brazil: data and child safety law
    • LGPD, the data-protection law: the child's best interest must prevail (ANPD guidance, 2023).
    • ECA Digital (Law 15.211/2025, in force March 2026): privacy by default and reliable age verification.
    • Sources: https://www.gov.br/anpd/pt-br/assuntos/noticias/anpd-divulga-enunciado-sobre-o-tratamento-de-dados-pessoais-de-criancas-e-adolescentes; https://www.planalto.gov.br/ccivil_03/_ato2023-2026/2025/lei/l15211.htm
  • England: Department for Education product safety expectations for generative AI (Jan 2025). https://www.gov.uk/government/publications/generative-ai-product-safety-expectations
  • Australian Framework for Generative AI in Schools (2023). https://www.education.gov.au/schooling/resources/australian-framework-generative-artificial-intelligence-ai-schools
  • Human Rights Watch (2022; Strong). 89% of government-endorsed edtech products reviewed surveilled children or could. https://www.hrw.org/report/2022/05/25/how-dare-they-peep-my-private-life/childrens-rights-violations-governments
  • Council of International Schools (CIS). Wellbeing is now an accreditation dimension, and CIS runs a safeguarding development programme. https://www.cois.org/about-cis/news/post/~board/news/post/announcement-thenew-cis-international-accreditation-framework-process

Board question: Do we know every AI tool touching student data, its risk tier, and who approved it?

C9. Strategy and evidence culture

The shift. The strongest institutions:

  • define learning first, then choose technology;
  • co-design strategy with teachers, students and families;
  • pilot and evaluate before scaling;
  • check who benefits, broken down by group;
  • publish their policies;
  • use accreditation as their strategic engine rather than running it in parallel.

Stages

  • Starting: strategy written by leadership alone.
  • Developing: community consultation.
  • Leading: co-authored, public, revisable strategy; small internal trials; AI support targeted where it helps most; accreditation cycle and strategic plan as one.

Anti-patterns

  • Believing pilot effect sizes at scale (Kraft: effects fall from about 0.44 to about 0.16 SD).
  • Vendor-authored evidence taken at face value.
  • A strategy that sits in a binder.

Evidence and exemplars

  • CIS International Accreditation Framework (Nov 2024). "Guided choice" lets schools lead development in areas they choose, and wellbeing is one of three dimensions. https://www.cois.org/about-cis/news/post/~board/news/post/announcement-thenew-cis-international-accreditation-framework-process
  • NEASC ACE Learning Principles. Each school writes its own "Shared Understanding of High-Quality Learning". Western Academy of Beijing publishes how it measures impact against them. https://www.neasc.org/resources-members/resources-international/ace-learning-principles; https://learn.wab.edu/faculty/impact/ace
  • Cognita AI (June 2025; Emerging). Piloted in six schools before a network-wide rollout, paired with teacher training and family engagement. https://www.cognita.com/news-and-views/launch-of-cognita-ai-global-roll-out-of-cutting-edge-ai-platform-to-support-teaching-learning/
  • Counter-examples
    • South Korea's AI textbooks were rushed and rolled out without teachers on board, and reversed within months (Rest of World, Oct 2025). https://restofworld.org/2025/south-korea-ai-textbook/
    • California State University gave ChatGPT to 460,000 students; its own survey of 94,000 found 65% of students doubt AI helps learning. https://www.insidehighered.com/news/tech-innovation/artificial-intelligence/2026/04/02/despite-skepticism-widespread-ai-use-cal
  • Brookings "Prosper, Prepare, Protect" (Jan 2026). Concludes that currently "the risks... overshadow its benefits" for children, and contrasts AI-enhanced with AI-diminished learning. https://www.brookings.edu/articles/a-new-direction-for-students-in-an-ai-world-prosper-prepare-protect/
  • OECD Digital Education Outlook 2026. Argues for "pedagogical intent" and for purpose-built educational AI. https://www.oecd.org/en/publications/oecd-digital-education-outlook-2026_062a7394-en.html

1. Themes

Theme 1: AI literacy becomes compulsory, as part of the curriculum rather than a ban

The shift. Between 2023 and 2026, whole systems moved from "should we allow ChatGPT?" to making AI literacy a required part of the curriculum from the early years. The leading approaches treat AI as a subject to study: how it works, bias, hallucination and ethics. They also treat it as a tool, with age limits on independent use. International benchmarks are catching up. OECD PISA 2029 will assess "Media & AI Literacy" (MAIL), and the OECD/European Commission AILit framework (review draft May 2025) is being written to feed into it. Brazil now has a national framework for AI in education, classified by risk.

Exemplars:

  • UAE Ministry of Education. AI is a mandatory subject from kindergarten to Grade 12 in government schools from 2025–26. It covers seven domains, from foundations to ethics and policy, and is built into the existing computing course with no extra hours. Announced 4 May 2025. https://www.uaebarq.ae/en/2025/05/04/ministry-of-education-introduces-ai-curriculum-in-public-schools-starting-from-2025-2026-academic-year/ ; https://gulfnews.com/uae/education/uae-to-launch-ai-curriculum-starting-in-kindergarten-7-core-concepts-revealed-1.500115415
  • China (Ministry of Education guidelines, May 2025; Beijing).
  • At least 8 class hours of AI a year from September 2025 in Beijing's roughly 1,400 schools.
  • Primary pupils may not use generative AI on their own.
  • Teachers may not hand their core teaching duties over to AI.
  • Sources (Global Times, May 2025; China Daily, 21 Oct 2025): https://www.globaltimes.cn/page/202505/1333878.shtml ; https://www.chinadaily.com.cn/a/202510/21/WS68f6d9bea310f735438b601e.html
  • Estonia, "AI Leap" (TI-Hüpe).
  • A public-private foundation giving 20,000 Grade 10–11 students and 3,000 teachers access to AI tools from September 2025.
  • Teachers are trained first.
  • Vocational students are added in September 2026.
  • The tool is designed to guide thinking rather than hand out answers.
  • Ministry of Education, 25 Feb 2025: https://www.hm.ee/en/news/estonia-announces-groundbreaking-national-initiative-ai-leap-programme-bring-ai-tools-all ; https://tihupe.ee/en/
  • Gwinnett County Public Schools (Georgia, USA).
  • A district-wide "AI-Ready" framework in six strands, deliberately including "human-only skills" such as ethics and creative problem-solving.
  • A three-course AI pathway.
  • An AI-themed high school (Seckinger).
  • Sources: EdSurge, 24 Feb 2025, https://www.edsurge.com/news/2025-02-24-how-to-develop-an-effective-framework-for-ai-readiness ; https://www.gcpsk12.org/programs-and-services/college-and-career-development/academies-and-career-technical-and-agricultural-education/artificial-intelligence-and-computer-science
  • Brazil:
  • Piauí state. AI has been a compulsory subject in Grade 9 and secondary school since 2024: more than 120,000 students, about 800 trained teachers, 540 schools. These are government figures (O Povo/G1, 1 Dec 2025). https://www.opovo.com.br/noticias/brasil/2025/12/01/como-a-inteligencia-artificial-virou-disciplina-obrigatoria-para-alunos-no-piaui.html
  • National Education Council (CNE). Approved risk-tiered AI guidelines on 1 Sept 2026. They take effect only once the Ministry of Education (MEC) formally ratifies them ("homologation"), which is still pending. Schools then have 12 months to implement. The tiers:
  • Low risk: organising materials, accessibility.
  • Moderate: tutoring and feedback, which need documentation and human review.
  • High: automated grading, biometric proctoring and profiling, which need an impact assessment.
  • Prohibited: emotional surveillance, social scoring, and automated pass/fail decisions.
  • AI literacy is required as learning content. There is no independent pupil access in the early grades.
  • Source: Revista Educação, 4 Sept 2026. https://revistaeducacao.com.br/2026/09/04/cne-aprovou-diretrizes-de-ia-na-educacao/
  • BNCC Computação. The computing strand of Brazil's national curriculum is mandatory from 2026 (Fundação Lemann). https://fundacaolemann.org.br/noticias/bncc-computacao/

Evidence strength: Anecdotal for outcomes. These are policy mandates, and no system has yet published data on what pupils learned.

Tensions and risks:

  • Mandates can outrun teacher capacity. In a Fundação Itaú survey, 84% of Brazilian students had used AI, but only 32% had received school guidance (fieldwork Nov–Dec 2024). https://www.fundacaoitau.org.br/noticias/educacao/84-dos-alunos-e-79-dos-professores-ja-utilizaram-ferramentas-de-ia-diz-estudo
  • "AI literacy" can shrink to tool training.

Board question: By 2027, can we show what our students know about how AI works and where it fails, not just which tools they can use? And are we ready for the CNE risk tiers once MEC ratifies them?

Theme 2: AI tutoring works when it is structured and teacher-mediated, and can harm learning when it is not

The shift. The strongest evidence of 2024–26 comes from randomised trials, and it points one way. AI tutoring produces large gains when it sits inside a structured programme, with teacher facilitation and prompts that push reasoning rather than give answers. Unguarded chatbot access can raise practice scores while lowering real learning. Forward-looking schools are therefore designing guardrails, not just buying licences.

Exemplars:

  • World Bank, Edo State, Nigeria.
  • A six-week after-school programme in 2024. Students worked in pairs with GPT-4 (via Microsoft Copilot), with a teacher present and reasoning-focused prompts.
  • Learning gains of about 0.3 standard deviations (SD), described as roughly 1.5–2 years of typical learning.
  • Better end-of-year exam results, including on topics outside the programme.
  • It outperformed about 80% of comparable interventions.
  • Policy Research Working Paper 11125, May 2025: https://eric.ed.gov/?id=ED676624 ; blog: https://blogs.worldbank.org/en/education/From-chalkboards-to-chatbots-Transforming-learning-in-Nigeria
  • Rori, Ghana.
  • A WhatsApp maths tutor, used for two 30-minute sessions a week over 8 months, by about 1,000 students in Grades 3–9.
  • Effect size 0.37, at about US$5 per student (arXiv, Feb 2024).
  • A full-scale trial with about 8,100 students is under way, with no results yet (J-PAL).
  • https://arxiv.org/abs/2402.09809 ; https://www.povertyactionlab.org/initiative-project/scaling-ai-powered-math-tutoring-across-diverse-educational-contexts-full-scale
  • Tutor CoPilot (Stanford), USA. AI coached human tutors in real time rather than replacing them.
  • A trial with more than 700 tutors and 1,000 students.
  • Students were 4 percentage points more likely to master topics, and 9 points more with the weakest tutors.
  • Cost about US$20 per tutor per year.
  • Oct 2024: https://edworkingpapers.com/ai24-1054
  • The cautionary trial: Bastani et al., a Turkish high school (PNAS, June 2025).
  • With unguarded GPT-4 access, practice scores rose by 48%.
  • When access was removed, students scored 17% below students who never had it.
  • A tutor version that gave teacher-designed hints avoided most of this harm.
  • https://www.pnas.org/doi/10.1073/pnas.2422633122
  • The cautionary scale-up: Alpha School, USA. An "AI tutor for 2 hours a day" private school network, expanding to about 50 campuses.
  • Its claims (2.6× growth on MAP tests) have not been independently verified. Scientific American, 28 Aug 2026: https://www.scientificamerican.com/article/alpha-schools-ai-teaching-model-is-expanding-does-it-work/
  • Unbound Academy, an Arizona charter using the model without selective admissions, reportedly reached 10% maths and 28% English proficiency in 2025. This comes from secondary commentary (The Economy of Meaning, 1 Sept 2026) and I have not checked it against Arizona's state data. [treat as emerging/unverified] https://theeconomyofmeaning.com/2026/09/01/do-we-know-the-alpha-and-omega-of-alpha-school/

Evidence strength:

  • Strong (several RCTs) that structured, guardrailed AI tutoring can produce large gains, mostly in low-resource settings.
  • Strong that unguarded access can reduce learning.
  • Anecdotal for "AI replaces the teacher" models.

Tensions and risks:

  • Most positive trials are in under-resourced contexts. The gains may be smaller in a well-resourced international school.
  • Students outsourcing their thinking to the tool.
  • Vendor claims that go beyond the data.

Board question: For every student-facing AI tool we adopt, what stops it doing the thinking for the student, and how would we know if it were?

Theme 3: AI for teacher workload, with the saved time put back into relationships

The shift. Leading schools give teachers institutional, privacy-compliant AI access and training first. They treat saved time as something to reinvest deliberately, for example in feedback and in knowing students well.

Exemplars:

  • Education Endowment Foundation (EEF), England.
  • A randomised trial with 259 Key Stage 3 science teachers in 68 schools, using ChatGPT with a guide.
  • Planning time fell by 31% (25 minutes a week).
  • Blind expert review found no drop in resource quality.
  • Dec 2024: https://educationendowmentfoundation.org.uk/news/teachers-using-chatgpt-alongside-a-guide-to-support-them-to-use-it-effectively-can-cut-lesson-planning-time-by-over-30-per-cent
  • Gallup / Walton Family Foundation, USA.
  • A survey of 2,232 public school teachers.
  • Weekly AI users (32%) report saving 5.9 hours a week, about six weeks a year.
  • 25 June 2025: https://news.gallup.com/poll/691967/three-teachers-weekly-saving-six-weeks-year.aspx
  • Colégio Bandeirantes, São Paulo.
  • Published a "Manual de Boas Práticas" (good-practice manual) for AI on 10 Feb 2025, co-built by the technology and teaching teams.
  • Discussion started in 2022, with a faculty workshop in July 2024.
  • Press reports that all teachers have institutional Copilot and Gemini access and that the approach complies with Brazil's data-protection law (LGPD).
  • https://colband.net.br/digiband/conheca-o-manual-de-boas-praticas-para-o-uso-da-ia/ ; manual PDF: https://colband.net.br/wp-content/uploads/2025/02/manual-inteligencia-artificial-2025a.pdf

Evidence strength: Strong for time saved on planning (the EEF trial). Emerging for the broader survey-based time savings, which are self-reported. No evidence yet that saved time turns into better student outcomes.

Tensions and risks:

  • Saved time gets absorbed by new demands.
  • A split between confident and non-using staff.
  • Data protection (LGPD in Brazil).

Board question: If AI gives each teacher back several hours a week, what have we decided they will do with it?

Theme 4: Assessment redesign in response to AI

The shift. Take-home essays and coursework no longer prove authorship. Leading schools are moving to:

  • declared "levels" of permitted AI use for each task;
  • more process evidence (drafts, conferences);
  • oral defence and in-class performance.

Exam systems are also shrinking and changing.

Exemplars:

  • AI Assessment Scale (Perkins, Roe and Furze).
  • A five-level scale from "No AI" to "AI exploration", revised in 2025.
  • Reported use in more than 350 institutions and translation into about 30 languages (author-reported).
  • Journal of University Teaching and Learning Practice, 22(7), 2025: https://leonfurze.com/wp-content/uploads/2025/09/JUTLPFinalPerkins_JUTLP_2025.pdf
  • International Baccalaureate (IB).
  • Does not ban AI. AI use must be cited under its academic-integrity policy.
  • Moving to digital exams in some Diploma Programme (DP) and Career-related Programme (CP) subjects from 2026.
  • https://ibo.org/programmes/artificial-intelligence-ai-in-learning-teaching-and-assessment/ ; https://www.ibo.org/programmes/diploma-programme/assessment-and-exams/
  • England Curriculum and Assessment Review (Nov 2025).
  • Recommends cutting GCSE exam time by at least 10%.
  • The government accepted most recommendations.
  • Summary: https://schoolsweek.co.uk/curriculum-review-all-the-key-policy-recommendations/
  • Contractor and Reyes (arXiv, July 2026). An undergraduate experiment, not K-12, but directly useful for task design.
  • Students who used AI to explain concepts ("augmentation") kept their gains.
  • Students who used it to generate text ("automation") lost their gains once AI was removed.
  • https://arxiv.org/abs/2607.08849

Evidence strength: Emerging. There is plenty of frameworks, but little outcome data at school level.

Tensions and risks:

  • Surveillance and detection arms races. AI detectors are unreliable, and Brazil's CNE classes biometric proctoring as high-risk.
  • Workload for teachers.
  • Inconsistency between departments.

Board question: Which of our current assessments still show what a student can do alone, and which have we just not re-examined yet?

Theme 5: Portrait of a graduate, competency-based learning and alternative transcripts

The shift. Schools and systems are defining a community-agreed "portrait" or "profile" of a graduate. The best ones then do the harder part: redesigning credit, assessment and transcripts around it. AI adds urgency, because the distinctively human competencies are what a transcript of grades cannot show.

Exemplars:

  • New York State Board of Regents.
  • Adopted a Portrait of a Graduate on 14 July 2025, with six qualities including "reflective and future-focused".
  • Regents exams stop being a graduation requirement from fall 2027. Proficiency can instead be shown through projects, debates and service learning.
  • Fully phased in by 2029–30.
  • Chalkbeat, 14 July 2025 and 4 Nov 2024: https://www.chalkbeat.org/newyork/2025/07/14/new-york-board-of-regents-exam-portrait-graduate-requirements/
  • Mastery Transcript Consortium (MTC).
  • Bought by ETS in May 2024 to scale a skills transcript.
  • More than 370 member schools. Its Mastery Record is accepted by more than 900 colleges.
  • The membership and acceptance figures are MTC's own and I have not checked them; the ETS purchase is confirmed by ETS.
  • https://www.ets.org/news/press-releases/ets-mastery-transcript-consortium-mtc-join-forces-scale-skills-transcript.html ; https://mastery.org/
  • Western Academy of Beijing (international school).
  • Is piloting MTC's Mastery Learning Record.
  • Runs "WAB Learning Futures 2025–27", including alumni profiles about competencies and pathways.
  • https://learn.wab.edu/ws/strategy/alumniprofile/resources
  • New Hampshire and Utah (USA).
  • New Hampshire: statewide competency-based graduation, and performance assessment moving from its earlier PACE programme to the current PLACE initiative.
  • Utah: portrait-aligned competency grants to about half of its districts.
  • https://nhlearninginitiative.org/place/from-pace-to-place-how-new-hampshire-is-redefining-school-accountability-through-classroom-aligned-innovation/ ; https://www.schools.utah.gov/curr/pcbl

Evidence strength:

  • Anecdotal/emerging for student outcomes.
  • Strong that adoption is real: AASA (the US superintendents' association) absorbed Battelle for Kids on 1 July 2025 to scale portrait work. https://www.aasa.org/news-media/news/2025/03/06/aasa-and-battelle-for-kids-announce-strategic-integration-to-expand-future-ready-learning-efforts-for-public-k-12-students

Tensions and risks:

  • Portraits that stay posters, never changing assessment.
  • University admissions acceptance. This matters for ISC families aiming at US, UK and Brazilian universities, including the vestibular entrance exams.
  • Validity and reliability of competency judgements.

Board question: If our graduate profile is real, where on a student's record does it show up, and would a university or employer believe it?

Theme 6: Real-world, project-based and interdisciplinary learning, with student agency

The shift. Leading schools are expanding authentic work: projects, internships, exhibitions and community partners. Rigorous evidence now shows that well-designed project-based learning (PBL) can raise, not dilute, academic results.

Exemplars:

  • Knowledge in Action (Lucas Education Research / USC).
  • A randomised trial of project-based AP Government and AP Environmental Science in five mostly urban districts.
  • AP pass rates were 8 points higher in year one and about 10 points higher in year two.
  • Published Feb 2021, but it remains the benchmark study.
  • https://www.lucasedresearch.org/research/knowledge-in-action/
  • Big Picture Learning (international network, 200+ schools).
  • Students learn through internships with workplace mentors.
  • A longitudinal study (Arnold and Mihut, Teachers College Record, 2020) found 74% of working alumni got jobs through internship contacts.
  • https://education-reimagined.org/findings-from-the-big-picture-learning-longitudinal-study/
  • Canopy project (CRPE and Transcend).
  • Tracks 319 innovative US schools.
  • Most have adopted new practices in wellbeing, deeper learning and postsecondary pathways since 2019.
  • https://crpe.org/projects/the-canopy-project/
  • Colégio Bandeirantes. A history project in which students rebuilt Pero Vaz de Caminha's 1500 letter as AI-generated video. An example of AI inside disciplinary inquiry (press report, date unverified). https://jornaldobras.com.br/noticia/119192/

Evidence strength: Strong for well-structured PBL (the trial). Emerging for internship models.

Tensions and risks:

  • Weak PBL becomes "activity without learning".
  • Scheduling and supervision capacity.
  • Equity of access to industry placements.

Board question: What share of a student's week involves work that matters to someone outside the school?

Theme 7: Phones, wellbeing and human skills as the counterweight to AI

The shift. From 2024 to 2026, phone restrictions went mainstream, including a national law in Brazil. At the same time, schools are strengthening the relational core of school: oracy (spoken language), adult-student relationships and belonging. Evidence on bans is mixed. Their gains show up mostly in school climate and focus, not in overall wellbeing. Meanwhile AI companions are a new wellbeing front.

Exemplars:

  • Brazil, Law 15.100 (13 Jan 2025).
  • Bans student phone use in class and at breaks in public and private schools.
  • Exceptions: teaching purposes, emergencies, accessibility and health.
  • Library of Congress, 13 Feb 2025: https://www.loc.gov/item/global-legal-monitor/2025-02-13/brazil-new-law-prohibits-cell-phone-use-in-schools/
  • A parliamentary education caucus (Frente Parlamentar Mista da Educação) survey with Equidade.info reported mixed effects [unverified: source not opened]:
  • 88% of students said they paid more attention.
  • 44% said they were more bored at break.
  • 49% of teachers saw more student anxiety.
  • Netherlands (national classroom phone ban since 1 Jan 2024).

Theme 8: New school structures, including staffing, teams, microschools and spaces

The shift. Instead of one teacher per classroom, leading systems use teams of educators with differentiated roles, and small learning communities. The drivers are teacher shortages, retention and personalisation. AI makes these designs more feasible.

Exemplars:

  • Opportunity Culture (Public Impact, USA).
  • Multi-classroom leaders run teams of teachers.
  • Two third-party studies across seven years found students gained close to an extra half-year of learning.
  • Reached about 275,000 students in 2025–26.
  • 24 Mar 2026 (organisation's own summary of the AIR/CALDER and Texas Tech studies): https://www.opportunityculture.org/2026/03/24/new-research-2-3x-schoolwide-high-growth-learning-nationally-high-growth-by-teaching-teams-with-opportunity-culture-staffing-design/
  • Mesa Public Schools with ASU's Next Education Workforce (Arizona).
  • Team teaching in 40+ schools.
  • Early data show better teacher retention and evaluation scores. Student outcome data are not yet published.
  • CRPE, May 2024: https://crpe.org/early-evidence-of-improved-educator-outcomes-in-next-education-workforcetm-models/
  • Microschools (USA).
  • National Microschooling Center sector analysis, May 2025, surveyed 800 microschools.
  • A later i-Ready snapshot of about 400 students in 27 microschools showed typical-or-better growth.
  • https://microschoolingcenter.org/news-blog/2025-american-microschools-sector-analysis ; The 74: https://www.the74million.org/article/microschool-students-keep-pace-academically-on-national-test-new-data-shows/

Tensions and risks:

  • Change fatigue.
  • Weak roles with no clear career path.
  • Microschool quality varies widely.

Board question: Is our staffing model designed for how we want students to learn, or inherited?

Theme 9: How leading schools run strategy, with community co-design, stated guardrails and futures thinking

The shift. Good AI and future-of-learning strategy is being built with teachers, students and families, not handed down. It is set out in public, revisable documents and paced to capacity. The Korean failure is the counter-example.

Exemplars:

  • Colégio Bandeirantes. A manual co-written by faculty, developed over three years (2022–25), plus student discussions and public "Band IA Talks" events. https://colband.net.br/digiband/band-ia-talks/
  • New York State. Its Blue Ribbon Commission and regional stakeholder process led to the Portrait of a Graduate (2024–25). See the Chalkbeat links in Theme 5.
  • Estonia. A public-private AI Leap Foundation, teacher preparation before student access, and phased rollout. See Theme 1.
  • Counter-example: South Korea's AI digital textbooks.
  • Launched March 2025 at a cost of 1.2 trillion won in public funds.
  • Development was compressed: 12 months against the usual 18, and 3 months of review against the usual 9.
  • Teachers reported more work, not less, and the teachers' union sued.
  • Stripped of textbook status in August 2025. Adoption fell from 37% to 19% of schools.
  • Rest of World, 15 Oct 2025: https://restofworld.org/2025/south-korea-ai-textbook/

Evidence strength: Anecdotal, although the Korean case is a well-documented natural experiment in what fails.

Board question: Who co-authored our strategy, and when did students and teachers last change it?

3. Signals of what is coming next (2027 and beyond)

  • AI literacy will be measured internationally. PISA 2029 MAIL simulates social media and generative AI environments (https://www.oecd.org/en/about/projects/pisa-2029-media-and-artificial-intelligence-literacy.html). Expect schools to be benchmarked on it.
  • Brazilian compliance deadlines. MEC homologation of the CNE guidelines is expected by about November 2026, followed by a roughly 12-month implementation window. BNCC Computação has been mandatory since 2026. Documentation, risk assessment and AI literacy will be required, not optional.
  • Skills transcripts at scale. ETS/MTC, New York's shift in 2027–29, and the IB's move to digital exams point toward richer records and fewer high-stakes paper exams.
  • Hybrid "human + AI" instruction as the default design. Tutor CoPilot-style tools support teachers rather than replace them. Large replication trials are pending, such as the Rori trial with about 8,100 Ghanaian students.
  • Regulation of AI companions and youth online safety is spreading. Australia is the test case. Early evidence suggests bans leak, so the role of schools in relationships and digital wellbeing grows.
  • Staffing redesign. Team-based roles, possibly with AI-supported paraprofessionals, as shortages persist.
  • Ongoing scrutiny of AI-first private models. Alpha is expanding to about 50 US campuses in 2026, with independent evaluation still missing.

Appendix B. Higher education

Higher Education Reference Framework, 2023–2026: What Leading Universities Are Doing

Scope: higher education worldwide, 2023 to September 2026. Every example below comes from a source I found during this research, and each one has a URL and date. Evidence ratings: Strong means randomized or large controlled evidence, or large representative surveys. Emerging means small RCTs, preprints, quasi-experiments or single-site studies. Anecdotal means announcements, press reports or institutional self-reports.

Caveat on method: my web search budget ran out near the end of the research. A few items therefore rest on search-result summaries plus one fetched page rather than two independent sources. They are marked "[single-source]". I did not include anything I could not trace to a named source.

1. Themes

Theme 1: Institution-wide AI provision (whole-system AI licences are now normal)

The shift. Between 2023 and 2025, universities moved from banning AI to tolerating it. Since 2025, leading institutions have been buying enterprise AI for every student and staff member. The main reasons they give are equal access (so paying students don't get an advantage), data protection, and signalling that the institution is "AI-ready". The field is now asking a different question: provision has not produced agreement that AI helps education, and contracts, costs and faculty consent are being argued over openly.

Exemplars

  • California State University (23 campuses). Rolled out ChatGPT Edu to more than 460,000 students and 63,000+ faculty and staff. It was announced as the "largest implementation of ChatGPT by any single organization". It is part of an "AI-empowered university" partnership with OpenAI, Google, Microsoft, AWS, NVIDIA, Adobe, LinkedIn and others. Announced February 2025 (Campus Technology, 5 Feb 2025; OpenAI).
  • CSU's own survey of fall 2025 drew 94,060 respondents. 95% had used at least one AI tool, but 65% of students and 59% of faculty doubt AI benefits education overall, and 18% of faculty forbid student AI use (Inside Higher Ed, 2 Apr 2026).
  • The original contract was about $17M. EdSource reported a renewal of about $13M a year for three years amid faculty criticism (EdSource, 2026; NPR, 25 May 2026). [Contract figures single-source: from search summaries, because the EdSource page blocked direct access.]
  • University of Oxford. First UK university to give ChatGPT Edu (GPT-5) to all staff and students. This followed a year-long pilot with about 750 researchers and staff, within a five-year OpenAI partnership (Oxford, 19 Sep 2025).
  • Anthropic "Claude for Education". Launched with LSE, Northeastern and Champlain College. It includes a "learning mode" that guides students' reasoning instead of giving answers (Anthropic, 2 Apr 2025).
  • Other campus-wide deals: Duke gives ChatGPT Edu to all members alongside its self-hosted DukeGPT (Duke, May 2025). Arizona State gives ChatGPT Edu to all students and staff (ASU).
  • Tecnológico de Monterrey (Mexico), the closest Latin American parallel. Built its own platform, TECgpt (launched to the Tec community September 2023). It created the AI Global Education Network (AIGEN) to share TECgpt with other universities, starting with a 10-university pilot (GlobeNewswire, 29 Jan 2025; TecScience).

Evidence strength. Adoption: strong, from large surveys. Learning impact of blanket provision: anecdotal, since nobody has published outcome data for system-wide licences.

Tensions and risks

  • Vendor lock-in and recurring costs during budget cuts. The AAUP and the Academe Blog frame CSU's spend as "AI centrism" at the expense of instruction (AAUP, Spring 2026; Academe Blog, 4 Jun 2026).
  • Faculty scepticism.
  • Access without pedagogy.

K-12 implication. Many students will arrive at university with an institutional AI account on day one. Schools should decide whether they provide a governed tool themselves or leave access to whoever can pay. Estonia's AI Leap is the national K-12 precedent: ChatGPT Edu and Claude for 20,000 upper-secondary students and 3,000 teachers from September 2025 (TI-Hüpe).

Theme 2: AI literacy or "fluency" as a graduation expectation for every student

The shift. Universities are moving from optional workshops to AI competence for every student in every discipline. This is often framed as "fluent in your field and in AI within your field". China is doing this through national policy. Western universities are doing it through general education requirements.

Exemplars

  • Ohio State University. Every undergraduate from the Class of 2029 is to graduate "AI fluent". GenAI basics are in the required General Education Launch Seminar and the First Year Success Series. Each college must produce a plan during 2025–26 (OSU News, June 2025).
  • Update: an AI Fluency Faculty Advisory Council was formed in November 2025. A faculty teaching endorsement comes with a $300 incentive. OSU plans 100 new AI-expert tenure-track faculty over five years (OSU OAA, 10 Nov 2025).
  • China: Ministry of Education and leading universities. Zhejiang University made an introductory AI course mandatory for undergraduates from 2024. April 2025 national guidelines raise AI to a "public foundational course" for all students (MIT Technology Review, 28 Jul 2025; China Daily, 25 Mar 2025).
  • Tsinghua issued university-wide Guiding Principles on AI in education. Instructors must define and disclose AI use per course. Students may use AI as a learning aid but must not copy or "mechanically paraphrase" AI output (Tsinghua via PR Newswire, 1 Dec 2025).
  • Russell Group (24 UK research universities). Its joint principles commit members to making students and staff "AI-literate" and adapting teaching and assessment (Russell Group, 3 Jul 2023).
  • Unicamp (Brazil). The University Council approved a GenAI resolution: AI is a support tool, not a substitute for human intellectual work. The university offered 40 courses with AI approaches in the first semester of 2026 and launched an AI portal and observatory (Unicamp, 19 Jun 2026).

Evidence strength. Anecdotal so far. These are requirements and plans, not measured outcomes.

Tensions. "Fluency" is poorly defined and can mean tool training rather than judgement. Students also report a gap: HEPI finds 68% of UK undergraduates see AI skills as essential, but under half feel staff prepare them (HEPI, 12 Mar 2026).

K-12 implication. Universities are building AI fluency on top of whatever students arrive with. Schools that teach discipline-specific AI judgement (verifying, critiquing, knowing when not to use it), rather than generic prompting, give students a head start.

Theme 3: Assessment redesign, from detection to "assurance of learning"

The shift. This is the most consequential change in the sector. Leading systems accept that unsupervised take-home work can no longer certify what a student knows. They are dividing assessment into two kinds. Secure assessment is supervised: in person, oral, or observed. Open assessment allows AI and is designed for learning. Assurance is increasingly planned across a whole degree rather than task by task. Oral exams and vivas are returning.

Exemplars

  • University of Sydney, "two-lane" approach. Lane 1 is secure, supervised assessment of learning, often "must-pass" hurdles. Lane 2 is open assessment, where AI "cannot be banned". Since Semester 1 2025 the default has been that AI is allowed except in exams and tests, and policy only permits AI restrictions in Lane 1 (Sydney, 27 Nov 2024; Teaching@Sydney FAQ).
  • TEQSA, Australia's national regulator. Enacting assessment reform in a time of AI sets out three pathways: assure learning at whole-program level, unit level, or a hybrid. It was written with UQ, Deakin, Monash, ECU, Sydney and UTS, and states that detection cannot guarantee integrity (TEQSA, Sep 2025).
  • Oral exams in the Ivy League and beyond.
  • Cornell uses 20-minute Socratic oral defences in biomedical engineering.
  • Penn's teaching centre describes "a massive shift toward in-person assessments".
  • NYU's Panos Ipeirotis built AI-powered oral exams.
  • UC San Diego launched a three-year study on scaling oral exams.
  • Source: Fortune, 25 Mar 2026.
  • Retreat from AI detectors. Curtin University switched off Turnitin's AI-writing detection from 1 Jan 2026 and kept similarity checking (Curtin). Trackers list 50+ universities that have disabled or discouraged detectors. [Count from secondary trackers, not independently verified.]

Evidence strength. Policy adoption: strong. Oral exams improving learning or validity at scale: emerging (UCSD's study is ongoing).

Scale of the problem.

  • HEPI 2026: 94% of UK undergraduates use GenAI for assessed work. 12% paste AI text directly into submissions, up from 3% in 2024. 65% say assessment has changed significantly (HEPI, 12 Mar 2026).
  • Gallup/Lumina (US): 57% of students use AI weekly for coursework (Gallup, 1 Apr 2026).

Tensions. Oral and in-person assessment costs staff time and raises equity issues for anxious, neurodivergent and second-language students. "Open lane" tasks risk becoming assessments nobody trusts.

K-12 implication. This is the clearest signal for schools. Students will increasingly be judged by explaining and defending their thinking live, and by supervised performance. Schools should build oral defence, presentation, and work shown under observation into regular practice, and teach students to document their process when AI is permitted.

Theme 4: Evidence on AI tutoring. Design, not access, decides the result

The shift. The evidence has matured from opinion to randomized trials. The consistent finding: purpose-built, pedagogically constrained AI tutors can produce large gains, while unrestricted general chatbots can inflate performance and reduce learning.

Exemplars

  • Harvard, Physical Sciences 2 (RCT, crossover, n=194). A custom AI tutor built on active-learning principles produced median learning gains more than double those of an in-class active learning session. The comparison was best practice, not a lecture (Kestin et al., Scientific Reports, June 2025). Strong but single-site and short-duration.
  • Wharton/Penn team, Turkish high school (RCT, n≈839).
  • During practice, GPT-4 access raised performance by 48% with the base chatbot and 127% with the tutor version.
  • On the later exam without AI, the base chatbot group scored 0.19 SD worse than controls. The hint-only "GPT Tutor" group showed no measurable harm (−0.01 SD).
  • Source: Bastani et al., PNAS, June 2025. Strong. This is a high-school sample, which makes it directly relevant to schools.
  • Stanford Tutor CoPilot (RCT, 900 tutors, 1,800 K-12 students). AI coaching human tutors raised topic mastery by 4 percentage points, and by 9 points for the least-experienced tutors. It cost about $20 per tutor per year (Wang et al., 2024 working paper). Strong.
  • Georgia Tech Jill Watson. A course-grounded virtual TA with answers checked against course material. It answered correctly 78.7% of the time versus 30.7% for a generic OpenAI Assistant. An A/B deployment with 600+ OMSCS students linked it to greater teaching presence and better performance (Georgia Tech, 2 Sep 2025). Emerging.
  • Newer nuance (preprint). In an RCT with proctored undergraduates, AI access raised test scores by 0.27 SD, persisting a week later. Students who used AI to explain concepts gained. Students who used it to generate text lost their gains once AI was removed (Contractor & Reyes, arXiv, 9 Jul 2026). Emerging.
  • Cautionary.
  • MIT Media Lab's EEG study (n=54) reported weaker neural connectivity and poor recall of their own essays among ChatGPT users ("cognitive debt"). It is a preprint, has drawn published critique, and the sample is small (arXiv, June 2025). Emerging/contested.
  • A 2026 meta-analysis of GenAI in programming education found a small, non-significant effect (g=0.14) (arXiv, May 2026).
  • Broader meta-analyses report medium effects, e.g. g≈0.50 across 36 higher-education studies (Frontiers in Psychology, 2026). The underlying studies vary in quality, so treat these pooled numbers cautiously.

How students actually use AI. Anthropic's analysis of 574,740 academic conversations found about 47% were "direct", meaning answer- or content-seeking with minimal engagement (Anthropic, 8 Apr 2025).

K-12 implication. The Bastani result is the key message for any school board. Giving students an unrestricted chatbot can make practice scores look better while real learning falls. Schools should deploy tutor-mode tools that give hints rather than answers, and assess without AI to find out what students have actually learned.

Theme 5: Admissions. Back to verifiable signals, and structured, AI-resistant applications

The shift. As essays become unreliable, selective universities are putting more weight on signals that are harder to fake: standardized tests, structured questions, and demonstrated activity.

Exemplars

  • MIT, Dartmouth, Yale, Brown, Harvard reinstated SAT/ACT requirements in 2024. Dartmouth and Yale said tests help identify talented lower-income applicants (Harvard Crimson, 11 Apr 2024; Axios, 15 Feb 2024).
  • UCAS (UK). For 2026 entry, the single personal statement was replaced by three structured questions: why this course, how your studies prepared you, and experience outside education. The total is still 4,000 characters (UCAS).
  • Kaplan survey of 220 US admissions offices (Jul–Aug 2025). Only 2% allow GenAI to write essays, 30% ban it, 68% have no policy. 27% allow it for brainstorming (Kaplan, Nov 2025).

Evidence strength. Policy changes: strong. Claims that tests or structured prompts predict success better: emerging, since they rest on institution-internal studies such as Dartmouth's and on Opportunity Insights research.

Tensions. Test requirements may disadvantage students without test-prep access. Policies on AI in essays are inconsistent across institutions.

K-12 implication. International school students need rigorous externally validated results (IB/AP/SAT), a written voice that is authentically their own, and a record of real activities they can talk about in interview. Schools should teach students their target universities' AI-in-application rules explicitly.

Theme 6: Micro-credentials, modular funding and lifelong learning entitlements

The shift. Governments are rebuilding post-school funding around modules and returning adult learners, not only three- or four-year degrees taken at 18. This is partly driven by AI-related job change.

Exemplars

  • UK Lifelong Learning Entitlement. A loan entitlement equivalent to four years of study, usable up to age 60 and fundable module by module. Applications open September 2026 for courses from January 2027. It has been delayed several times (GOV.UK; House of Commons Library).
  • Singapore SkillsFuture Level-Up. Citizens aged 40+ receive a non-expiring S$4,000 credit (from May 2024), up to 90% subsidy for a second full-time diploma, and a training allowance (MOE Singapore, 4 Mar 2024).
  • Australia Microcredentials Pilot. A$18.5M. Round 2 funded 48 microcredentials at 25 providers. An independent evaluation is due mid-2026 (Dept of Education, 29 Aug 2024).
  • EU Council Recommendation on micro-credentials (16 Jun 2022, before this report's window but still the framework in force) (European Commission).
  • Competency-based degrees. Western Governors University has 180,000+ students in self-paced degrees that advance on demonstrated mastery (Forbes, 21 Jan 2025). Tec de Monterrey's Tec21 competency model dates from 2019 (Tec).

Evidence strength. Policy: strong. Labour-market value of micro-credentials: emerging, with evaluations pending.

Tensions. Credential inflation, uneven quality, and low take-up by the people who most need retraining.

K-12 implication. The "one degree for life" story is weakening. Schools should build students' habits of self-directed learning and the ability to show evidence of their skills (portfolios, projects), not just prepare them for entry exams.

Theme 7: The labour-market signal. Entry-level work is being reshaped first

The shift. Universities' main promise, employability, is under pressure at the graduate entry point.

Exemplars

  • Stanford Digital Economy Lab, "Canaries in the Coal Mine". Employment of 22–25-year-olds in the most AI-exposed occupations is now about 19% below trend relative to less-exposed peers, up from 13–15% in 2025. There is no comparable gap for experienced workers. The effect comes through reduced hiring, not firing, and appears where AI substitutes for workers, not where it complements them (Stanford DEL, 12 Aug 2026).
  • WEF Future of Jobs 2025 (1,000+ employers). Fastest-growing skills: AI and big data, networks and cybersecurity, tech literacy. Alongside them: creative thinking, resilience/flexibility/agility, and curiosity and lifelong learning (WEF, Jan 2025).
  • Student reaction. 16% of enrolled US students have already changed major because of AI (Gallup, 2026).

Evidence strength. Strong as descriptive data; causation is still debated.

Tension. It is uncertain whether the effect is AI or macroeconomic hiring conditions. Stanford controls for firm-level shocks.

K-12 implication. Routine codified knowledge is what AI replaces. Tacit, experiential and interpersonal capability holds its value. Schools should increase authentic, applied and collaborative work.

Theme 8: Faculty roles and development

The shift. Faculty are being asked to redesign courses, assess differently and model AI use, often with little support. The balance between augmentation and automation is also contested: AI assisting academics versus AI replacing their work.

Exemplars

  • EDUCAUSE 2025 AI Landscape Study (788 respondents). Only 22% of institutions have an institution-wide AI strategy. Faculty training is the top strategic element (63%). 68% say students use AI more than faculty (EDUCAUSE, 17 Feb 2025).
  • AAC&U Institute on AI, Pedagogy and the Curriculum. A national faculty development programme for campus teams: 124 institutions in 2024–25 and about 175 in 2025–26 (AAC&U).
  • Anthropic, how educators use Claude (74,000 conversations). 57% of use is curriculum development. Teaching tasks are 77% augmentation, but grading is 49% automation, even though faculty rated grading AI's least effective use. One professor: "Students are not paying tuition for the LLM's time, they're paying for my time" (Anthropic, 27 Aug 2025).
  • Governance flashpoint: ASU's "Atom". A $5-a-month Claude-based course builder was assembled from faculty lectures without telling the faculty. Professors called the result "Frankensteinian" (Inside Higher Ed, 29 Apr 2026).

Evidence strength. Strong for surveys. Anecdotal for development impact.

K-12 implication. Teacher development and teacher consent over AI use are what determine success, not the tool. Schools should agree policies on AI in grading and on reuse of teachers' own materials before deploying anything.

Theme 9: Wellbeing and the "human" value proposition

The shift. When content and tutoring are cheap and always available, campus education has to justify itself through mentorship, belonging, supervised practice and human judgement. Meanwhile, students are turning to AI for emotional support.

Exemplars

  • Student use of AI for emotional support. HEPI 2026: about 15% of UK undergraduates use AI for companionship or loneliness. Students report worries about isolation and losing skills (HEPI, Mar 2026). Jisc: students use AI for mental-health advice and want clearer guidance (Jisc, 21 May 2025).
  • Dartmouth Therabot. The first RCT of a purpose-built generative AI therapy bot, with 210 adults. Depression symptoms fell by 51% versus a waitlist. The authors stress clinical supervision is still needed (NEJM AI / Dartmouth, 27 Mar 2025). Strong for this trial, but the participants were adults, not a campus deployment.
  • Mental health is the main reason students consider leaving. Mental health and emotional stress remain the top reasons US students consider stopping out (Lumina–Gallup State of Higher Education).
  • Tsinghua's "Xiaoda". An AI "growth assistant" given to all incoming first-years for study and life guidance (Tsinghua). Anecdotal.

Tension. AI companionship could either help students or deepen isolation. Proof of what "human" campus value adds is thin: it is widely argued but rarely measured.

K-12 implication. Schools that visibly invest in relationships, advisory and mentoring, and in-person community are aligning with where higher education is heading. They also need explicit guidance for students on using AI for emotional support.

2. Short list for the Board

  1. Harvard's AI tutor beat active learning, more than doubling learning gains (RCT, 2025). But only because it was purpose-built pedagogically.
  2. Unrestricted GPT-4 made high-school students 0.19 SD worse on the exam without AI, even though practice scores rose. A tutor version that gave only hints avoided the harm (PNAS 2025). This is the single most important caution for schools.
  3. Cal State gave ChatGPT to 460,000 students. Its own survey of 94,000 people found 65% of students doubt it helps learning. Access alone is not a strategy.
  4. Sydney's "two-lane" assessment: secure in-person lane plus AI-open lane, now reflected in Australia's national regulator guidance (TEQSA 2025).
  5. Oral exams are back at Cornell, Penn and NYU (2026). Explaining your reasoning out loud is the new integrity test.
  6. Ohio State: every graduate from 2029 "AI fluent" in their own discipline. China made AI a foundational course for all university students.
  7. Young graduates in AI-exposed jobs: employment 19% below trend (Stanford, Aug 2026). Entry-level hiring is where the change shows first.
  8. Harvard, Yale, Dartmouth and MIT brought back SAT requirements. UCAS replaced the free-form personal statement. Admissions are moving toward signals that are hard to fake.

3. Signals for 2027 and beyond

These are forward-looking inferences drawn from the sources above, not established facts.

  • Degree-level assurance of learning becomes the norm. TEQSA's program-level pathway points to fewer, higher-stakes secure checkpoints (vivas, capstone defences) and AI-open coursework in between.
  • Scaled and AI-run oral assessment. NYU is already experimenting with AI voice examiners, and UCSD is studying how to scale oral exams. Expect disputes over the fairness of AI-administered vivas.
  • The debate moves from access to tutor design. Evidence consistently favours constrained tutors (Harvard PS2 Pal, Bastani's GPT Tutor, Anthropic's learning mode, Jill Watson). Procurement will increasingly specify pedagogical behaviour.
  • Modular, lifelong funding goes live. The UK Lifelong Learning Entitlement starts January 2027. Singapore and Australia models may be scaled or evaluated (Australia's pilot evaluation is due mid-2026).
  • AI-native and platform institutions. Examples include rebranded "AI universities" (e.g., Maestro University, Dec 2025, Wikipedia, anecdotal) and ASU-style products that sell course content directly. Expect more faculty IP and governance conflict.
  • A backlash and cost reckoning over AI licences (CSU renewal controversy). Institutions will be asked to show learning impact before renewing.
  • Entry-level labour pressure pushes universities toward experiential, apprenticeship-like and tacit-skill learning.
  • Latin America. Tec de Monterrey's shared TECgpt network and Unicamp's governance model suggest regional universities, including in Brazil, will formalise AI policy and curriculum through 2026–27. This is directly relevant to Curitiba families choosing Brazilian or Mexican universities.

Appendix C. Professional and workplace learning

Professional and workplace learning, and the world of work (2023 to Sept 2026): research report for UnconstrainED

Scope note: I used web search and fetch, which ran out after about 60 queries. Most claims below come from the primary source: the paper, press release or government page. Where I only found a figure in secondary coverage, I say so. I have not included vendor marketing statistics, such as "role-play lifts sales 27%". Evidence ratings are Strong (randomised or large-scale administrative data), Emerging (well-designed but a single study, or observational), and Anecdotal (company self-reports and announcements).

1. Themes

Theme 1: Organisations are moving from "AI training" to "AI fluency as a job expectation"

The shift: Between 2023 and 2026, leading employers stopped treating AI as an optional course. They made fluent use a baseline expectation, and some wrote it into performance reviews, headcount requests and hiring rubrics. Short mandatory training at national scale sits alongside this. The hard part turns out to be the organisation (managers, culture, workflow redesign), not the individual course.

Exemplars:

  • Shopify. CEO Tobi Lütke's memo said "Reflexive AI usage is now a baseline expectation." Teams must show why AI can't do a job before asking for headcount, and AI use is part of performance and peer reviews. Source: https://x.com/tobi/status/1909251946235437514 (7 Apr 2025); coverage at https://www.digitalcommerce360.com/2025/04/08/internal-memo-shopify-ceo-declares-ai-non-optional/
  • Citi. Uses a peer model of "AI Champions and Accelerators": about 4,000 volunteers across business units in 84 countries, and over 70% of about 182,000 staff reportedly using firm-approved AI tools. Secondary press only; I could not reach the primary source. https://www.artificialintelligence-news.com/news/the-quiet-work-behind-citi-4000-person-internal-ai-rollout/ (early 2026)
  • JPMorgan Chase. Its in-house "LLM Suite" reached about 200,000 users within eight months of its mid-2024 launch through opt-in, "viral" adoption, backed by role-specific training. Secondary reporting. https://www.artificialintelligence-news.com/news/jpmorgan-chase-ai-strategy-2025/ (2025)
  • Moderna. Merged HR and IT under one Chief People and Digital Technology Officer (Tracey Franklin, May 2025) to do "work planning" rather than "workforce planning". https://www.unleash.ai/artificial-intelligence/why-moderna-merged-hr-and-it-to-better-architect-the-flow-of-work/ (2025)
  • Governments. The UK "AI Skills Boost" aims to upskill 10 million workers by 2030. It reports 1 million courses delivered since June 2025, with free modules under 20 minutes; only 21% of UK workers feel confident using AI. https://www.gov.uk/government/news/free-ai-training-for-all-as-government-and-industry-programme-expands-to-provide-10-million-workers-with-key-ai-skills-by-2030 (28 Jan 2026). Singapore's Budget 2026 created a National AI Impact Programme to train 100,000 workers, with free premium AI tools for learners on selected SkillsFuture courses. https://www.content.mycareersfuture.gov.sg/budget-2026-singaporean-workers-employers (Feb 2026)

Evidence:

  • Microsoft 2026 Work Trend Index (20,000 AI-using workers in 10 markets, including Brazil). Organisational factors explain more than twice as much of reported AI impact as individual factors (67% vs 32%). When managers model AI use, employees report a 17-point lift in perceived AI value. https://www.microsoft.com/en-us/worklab/work-trend-index/agents-human-agency-and-the-opportunity-for-every-organization (5 May 2026)
  • BCG AI at Work 2025 (10,600 workers). 79% of employees with more than 5 hours of training were regular users, against 67% of those with less. Frontline use has stalled at 51%, and only 36% feel adequately trained. https://www.bcg.com/publications/2025/ai-at-work-momentum-builds-but-gaps-remain (26 Jun 2025)
  • Rating: Emerging. These are large surveys, but they are self-reported, and in Microsoft's case vendor-run.

Tensions and risks:

  • "AI-first" mandates can backfire. Duolingo's April 2025 memo said it would "gradually stop using contractors to do work that AI can handle". After the backlash, CEO Luis von Ahn said "I did not give enough context." https://techcrunch.com/2025/08/17/duolingo-ceo-says-controversial-ai-memo-was-misunderstood (17 Aug 2025)
  • Some firms pair reskilling with exits. Accenture said it will "exit" staff for whom reskilling "is not a viable path", cutting about 11,000 roles. https://www.cnbc.com/2025/09/26/accenture-plans-on-exiting-staff-who-cant-be-reskilled-on-ai.html (26 Sep 2025)
  • Pilots often fail. MIT Project NANDA claims 95% of enterprise GenAI pilots showed no measurable P&L return. It was not peer reviewed and had a small interview base. https://www.forbes.com/sites/jasonsnyder/2025/08/26/mit-finds-95-of-genai-pilots-fail-because-companies-avoid-friction/ (Aug 2025)

Implication for schools:

  • Staff: Fluency is a culture and leadership problem more than a course problem. Leaders need to model use, set clear policy and protect time for experimenting, and a peer-champion network is the proven pattern.
  • Graduates: They will arrive at employers where AI use is assumed and assessed. They need to have used AI habitually, and be able to say when not to.
Theme 2: AI coaches, simulations and role-play are becoming mainstream in L&D, with learning in the flow of work

The shift: L&D is moving from publishing courses to "enablement". That means AI conversation simulators for practising difficult conversations, AI copilots that coach in real time during the work, and AI-generated, personalised content.

Exemplars:

  • Bank of America Academy. Employees completed over 1 million AI conversation simulations in a year to practise client interactions, and more than 90% of employees use the "Erica for Employees" assistant. https://www.prnewswire.com/news-releases/ai-adoption-by-bofas-global-workforce-improves-productivity-client-service-302423333.html (2025)
  • Stanford "Rehearsal" (research prototype). An LLM simulates a conflict partner and gives feedback. Against lecture-only training (N=40), participants cut escalating tactics by 67% and doubled cooperative ones in a later unaided conflict. https://dl.acm.org/doi/10.1145/3613904.3642159 (CHI, May 2024)
  • Tutor CoPilot (Stanford) shows real-time coaching of novices at work. In an RCT with 900 tutors and 1,800 K-12 students, students were 4 percentage points more likely to reach mastery, rising to 9 points for students of the least-experienced tutors. Tutors were also 10 percentage points more likely to prompt students to explain their thinking. https://nssa.stanford.edu/studies/tutor-copilot-human-ai-approach-scaling-real-time-expertise (Oct 2024)
  • Josh Bersin Company (industry analyst). Its research covers 800 organisations and describes a shift to "dynamic enablement". It finds 76% of organisations are stuck at the lowest learning maturity. https://joshbersin.com/2026/02/new-research-how-ai-transforms-400-billion-of-corporate-learning/ (Feb 2026)

Evidence:

  • Emerging for the design principle of simulated practice plus feedback, and for real-time copilots helping novices (Tutor CoPilot is strong but only one RCT).
  • Anecdotal for enterprise simulator ROI. BofA gives usage figures, not outcomes, and Bersin's correlations are not causal.

Tensions and risks:

  • Simulations can be unrealistic. Rehearsal worked partly because experts structured the prompting.
  • Coaching can breed dependence. See Theme 4.
  • Fidelity and bias in AI role-players are hard to check.

Implication for schools:

  • Staff: AI simulation is a ready-made tool for practising parent conversations, restorative conversations and feedback to students. Real-time copilots can raise the floor for novice teachers and tutors.
  • Graduates: Practising oral, interpersonal and negotiation skills against AI role-players can become a routine part of learning.
Theme 3: What the productivity evidence shows. Big gains inside the "jagged frontier", biggest for novices, and failures outside it

The shift: The strongest research base in this area is field experiments. Together they show three things:

  1. Large gains on tasks AI does well, with the biggest gains going to less-experienced workers.
  2. Worse performance when people over-trust AI on tasks it does badly.
  3. AI can act as a "teammate" that spreads expertise across functions.

Exemplars:

  • Harvard/BCG, "Navigating the Jagged Technological Frontier" (758 BCG consultants, RCT). On tasks inside the frontier, consultants with GPT-4 completed 12.2% more tasks, 25.1% faster, at more than 40% higher quality. On a task designed to sit outside the frontier, AI users did worse than the control group. https://papers.ssrn.com/sol3/papers.cfm?abstract_id=4573321 (Sept 2023)
  • Brynjolfsson, Li and Raymond, "Generative AI at Work" (5,179 support agents). Resolutions per hour rose 14% on average and 34% for novices and low-skilled agents, with little effect on experts. The AI spread the practices of top performers. Quarterly Journal of Economics 140(2), 2025. https://www.nber.org/papers/w31161
  • P&G "Cybernetic Teammate" (776 professionals, RCT). An individual with AI matched the performance of a two-person team without AI. AI also dissolved the gap between R&D-style and commercial-style proposals. https://www.nber.org/papers/w33641 (Mar 2025; reported as later published in Organization Science)
  • Microsoft/Harvard field experiment (7,137 workers in 66 firms). Regular users of the embedded AI spent 31% (3.6 hours) less time a week on email, but there was no change in meetings or in the mix of tasks. https://www.nber.org/papers/w33795 (2025)
  • METR on software developers. In early 2025, experienced open-source developers took 19% longer with AI while believing it made them 20% faster (https://metr.org/blog/2025-07-10-early-2025-ai-experienced-os-dev-study/, 10 Jul 2025). The late-2025 follow-up found roughly neutral to positive effects, and METR now concludes AI "likely provides productivity benefits in early 2026". It also found that 30-50% of developers refused to work without AI. https://metr.org/blog/2026-02-24-uplift-update/ (24 Feb 2026)

Evidence: Strong. These are multiple randomised or quasi-experimental studies in real workplaces.

Tensions and risks:

  • Perception gaps: people misjudge their own AI-assisted productivity.
  • Individual gains don't automatically become organisational gains.
  • Levelling of expertise helps novices now, but raises questions about how they will build expertise later (Theme 4).

Implication for schools:

  • Graduates need "frontier judgement": knowing which tasks AI is good or bad at, checking outputs, and noticing when it is confidently wrong. Schools can teach this explicitly with tasks designed to include both.
  • Staff: Expect the largest gains among early-career teachers, and design mentoring accordingly.
Theme 4: Deskilling and the "skill formation" problem

The shift: A growing body of evidence suggests that relying on AI can erode skills people already have, or stop them forming. How AI is used is the deciding factor.

Exemplars:

  • Lancet Gastroenterology & Hepatology (4 Polish centres). After routine AI-assisted colonoscopy was introduced, endoscopists' adenoma detection without AI fell from 28.4% to 22.4%. https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/40816301/ (Aug 2025). Observational.
  • Anthropic RCT on learning a new coding library (52 mostly junior engineers). The AI group scored 17% lower on an immediate comprehension quiz (50% vs 67%), with the biggest gap on debugging. People who used AI to ask for explanations and pose conceptual questions kept their mastery. https://www.anthropic.com/research/AI-assistance-coding-skills (late Jan 2026)
  • Microsoft Research and Carnegie Mellon survey (319 knowledge workers, CHI 2025). The more workers trusted AI, the less critical thinking they reported; the more they trusted their own skills, the more they checked. https://dl.acm.org/doi/full/10.1145/3706598.3713778 (Apr 2025)
  • Anthropic Economic Index, "Learning curves". Users with 6 or more months' experience are about 5 percentage points more likely to have successful conversations. They iterate and validate more, and delegate less. https://www.anthropic.com/research/economic-index-march-2026-report (24 Mar 2026)

Evidence: Emerging. There is one RCT on skill formation, one observational clinical study and surveys, but the findings point the same way.

Tensions and risks:

  • Speed now vs expertise later. Organisations optimise for short-term speed, while the loss of expertise shows up later and in other people's jobs.

Implication for schools: This is the most direct bridge to classroom practice.

  • Graduates: Students need to know how to learn with AI without outsourcing the learning: asking for explanations, attempting first, then checking. They also need to keep core skills they can use without AI.
  • Staff: Teachers need an assessment design that shows the difference between the two.
Theme 5: Entry-level work is being redesigned and the "bottom rung" is under pressure

The shift: The clearest labour-market signal of AI so far is fewer hires of young workers in AI-exposed, automatable occupations. Experienced workers are not affected in the same way. Some employers are responding by redesigning junior roles rather than cutting them.

Exemplars:

  • Stanford Digital Economy Lab, "Canaries in the Coal Mine" (ADP payroll data). Employment of 22-25-year-olds in highly AI-exposed jobs is now about 19% below that of less-exposed peers, up from about 15% in July 2025. The gap comes mainly from reduced hiring. Where AI complements workers, employment is flat or rising. https://digitaleconomy.stanford.edu/news/canariesaug26/ (12 Aug 2026); original paper at https://digitaleconomy.stanford.edu/publications/canaries-in-the-coal-mine (Aug 2025)
  • Hosseini and Lichtinger (Harvard), "Generative AI as Seniority-Biased Technological Change" (62 million workers). After firms adopt GenAI, junior employment falls relative to non-adopters while senior employment is unchanged, again driven by slower hiring. https://papers.ssrn.com/sol3/papers.cfm?abstract_id=5425555 (Sept 2025)
  • Burning Glass Institute, "No Country for Young Grads". 52% of the Class of 2023 were underemployed a year after graduating. It names an AI-driven "expertise upheaval" as one of four structural drivers. https://www.burningglassinstitute.org/research/no-country-for-young-grads (Jul 2025)
  • IBM (counter-example). IBM plans to triple US entry-level hiring in 2026 after rewriting junior roles. Junior developers now do less routine coding and more work with customers. Its CHRO said without entry-level hiring "the well simply dries up." https://www.axios.com/2026/02/13/ai-ibm-tech-jobs (13 Feb 2026)
  • PwC 2026 Global AI Jobs Barometer (more than 1 billion job ads in 27 countries). AI-exposed entry-level roles are seven times more likely to require traditionally senior skills (judgement, leadership, creativity, face-to-face work). Jobs requiring AI skills carry a wage premium. https://www.pwc.com/gx/en/news-room/press-releases/2026/pwc-2026-ai-jobs-barometer.html (15 Jun 2026)

Evidence: Strong to emerging. The administrative data is strong, but attributing the effect to AI rather than interest rates or over-hiring is still debated.

Tensions and risks:

  • The expertise pipeline problem. If juniors never do the "grunt work", how do they become experts?
  • Apprenticeship is the obvious answer, but mostly announced so far. One example is the US Department of Labor's April 2026 initiative to build AI skills into Registered Apprenticeships (https://www.dol.gov/newsroom/releases/eta/eta20260401, 1 Apr 2026). I could not fetch the page to confirm details.

Implication for schools:

  • Graduates will enter jobs that expect "senior" capacities earlier: judgement, client interaction, owning outcomes and managing AI output. Schools should build these through authentic projects, internships and real audiences, not only content mastery.
  • Careers guidance: Paths through apprenticeships and skills-based entry deserve more weight.
Theme 6: Skills-based hiring and new AI-fluency signals. The rhetoric runs ahead of practice

The shift: Employers say they value skills over degrees, and new credentials (AI certifications) and assessments (AI-allowed interviews) are appearing. Actual hiring changed slowly, but AI fluency is fast becoming an explicit hiring criterion.

Exemplars:

  • Harvard Business School and Burning Glass Institute. Despite widespread pledges to drop degree requirements, fewer than 1 in 700 hires in 2023 benefited. Only 37% of firms were "skills-based hiring leaders"; 45% changed in name only. https://www.hbs.edu/bigs/joseph-fuller-college-degree-gap (Feb 2024)
  • Canva. Expects engineering candidates to use AI tools (Copilot, Cursor, Claude) in technical interviews and scores how well they use them. https://www.theregister.com/2025/06/11/canva_coding_assistant_job_interviews/ (11 Jun 2025). Meta reportedly added an AI-enabled coding round in October 2025 (secondary sources only).
  • Zapier. Publishes an AI-fluency hiring rubric. "Capable", now the minimum bar, means AI is embedded in core work through repeatable systems with measurable impact. It also judges the candidate's "slope" (trajectory). https://zapier.com/blog/raising-ai-fluency-bar-in-hiring/ (2026 per secondary reports; exact date unverified)
  • OpenAI Certifications and Jobs Platform. Pledge to certify 10 million Americans by 2030, with Walmart offering free certification to its US associates. https://openai.com/index/expanding-economic-opportunity-with-ai/ (Sept 2025). This is an announcement, not an outcome.
  • WEF Future of Jobs 2025. 39% of core skills will change by 2030. Analytical thinking is the top core skill (7 in 10 employers). AI and big data are the fastest-growing skills, followed by resilience, flexibility, leadership, creative thinking and curiosity. The report projects 170 million jobs created and 92 million displaced. https://www.weforum.org/publications/the-future-of-jobs-report-2025/ (Jan 2025)

Evidence: Strong that pledges have outrun practice. The rise of AI-fluency hiring is at an early, anecdotal stage.

Tensions and risks:

  • Credential inflation: a proliferation of vendor badges.
  • Rubrics measure tool use more easily than judgement.

Implication for schools:

  • Graduates should leave with evidence of skills (portfolios, documented AI-assisted work showing their process and verification), not just grades.
  • Schools can also assess AI fluency the way Zapier and Canva do: judging how students direct the AI, check what it produces and explain their reasoning.
Theme 7: Upskilling the education workforce itself

The shift: Since 2024, teacher AI professional learning has moved from ad-hoc workshops to national frameworks, union-led academies and government courses. Classroom use is running ahead of guidance, and there is now randomised evidence that AI saves time without lowering quality.

Exemplars:

  • UNESCO AI Competency Framework for Teachers. 15 competencies across 5 dimensions (human-centred mindset, ethics, foundations, pedagogy, AI for professional learning), at 3 levels (Acquire, Deepen, Create). https://www.unesco.org/en/articles/ai-competency-framework-teachers (2024)
  • AFT National Academy for AI Instruction (US). A $23 million academy funded by Microsoft, OpenAI and Anthropic, aiming to train 400,000 educators over five years from a Manhattan hub. https://www.aft.org/press-release/aft-launch-national-academy-ai-instruction-microsoft-openai-anthropic-and-united (8 Jul 2025)
  • Brazil's Ministry of Education (MEC) (directly relevant to Curitiba).
  • Launched, with UNESCO, AI training for lower-secondary (anos finais) teachers: https://www.gov.br/mec/pt-br/assuntos/noticias/2026/junho/mec-lanca-curso-de-ia-para-professores-do-ensino-fundamental (Jun 2026)
  • Now offers nine free AI courses for educators, including a 180-hour "Formação de Professores em IA": https://www.gov.br/mec/pt-br/assuntos/noticias/2026/agosto/mec-oferece-nove-cursos-gratuitos-de-ia-para-professores (Aug 2026)
  • These are aligned to MEC's guidance on AI in basic education.
  • Estonia "AI Leap" (TI-Hüpe). Teachers received training and ChatGPT/Gemini access before students; more than 60% of participating teachers now use them weekly. Students got a custom learning app designed to "guide thinking rather than provide ready-made answers". The programme covers about 4,900 teachers in 154 schools with €4 million of public and private funding. https://tihupe.ee/en/estonia-launches-nationwide-ai-leap-education-program-and-introduces-learning-supportive-ai-in-upper-secondary-schools/ (2026)
  • EEF/NFER RCT (England) (259 teachers in 68 schools). ChatGPT plus a short guide cut lesson-planning time by 31% (about 25 minutes a week), with no loss of quality as judged by a blind expert panel. https://educationendowmentfoundation.org.uk/projects-and-evaluation/projects/choices-in-edtech-using-generative-ai-chatgpt-for-ks3-science-lesson-preparation-2024-teacher-choices-trial (Dec 2024)
  • Gallup and Walton Family Foundation.
  • Weekly AI users among teachers save about 5.9 hours a week (about six weeks a year), and schools with an AI policy see a larger "dividend": https://news.gallup.com/poll/691967/three-teachers-weekly-saving-six-weeks-year.aspx (Jun 2025)
  • By 2026, only 18% of US teachers had formal AI guidance and 34% had none: https://news.gallup.com/poll/710534/teachers-receive-no-formal-guidance.aspx (26 May 2026)
  • England's DfE publishes "Using AI in education settings" guidance and training modules, developed with the Chartered College of Teaching. https://www.gov.uk/government/publications/generative-artificial-intelligence-in-education/generative-artificial-intelligence-ai-in-education (updated 2025-26)

Evidence:

  • Strong for time savings on planning (the EEF RCT).
  • Emerging for time savings more generally (Gallup is self-reported).
  • Anecdotal for effects of AI professional learning on student outcomes. The notable exception is Tutor CoPilot.

Tensions and risks:

  • Vendor-funded training (AFT) raises questions about independence.
  • Policy vacuum: teachers are making consequential decisions, especially on grading and feedback, without guidance.
  • Time saved may simply be absorbed by other work.

Implication for schools:

  • Staff: Train teachers before students; pair tools with clear policy (policy increases the benefit); use peer-led professional learning; and adopt a competency framework (UNESCO is the obvious reference, and MEC's courses are free and in Portuguese).
  • School leaders: They need their own track on governance, data and assessment redesign.
Theme 8: Human skills rise in value: judgement, collaboration, communication, ethics

The shift: As AI takes on routine cognitive work, employers and the data put more value on judgement, quality control of AI output, leadership, interpersonal work and ethics. Service firms are also rediscovering the human touch as a premium product.

Exemplars:

  • Deming. Jobs needing strong social skills grew and earned higher wages, especially where social and maths skills combine. https://www.nber.org/digest/nov15/growing-importance-social-skills-labor-market (QJE 2017; synthesis updated by Deming and Silliman, June 2025: https://www.rfberlin.com/wp-content/uploads/2025/06/25020.pdf)
  • Microsoft 2026 Work Trend Index. Workers name quality control of AI output (50%) and critical thinking (46%) as increasingly important. https://www.microsoft.com/en-us/worklab/work-trend-index/agents-human-agency-and-the-opportunity-for-every-organization (May 2026)
  • Klarna. After claiming AI did the work of 700 agents (Feb 2024), the CEO said cost had been "a too predominant evaluation factor… what you end up having is lower quality", and Klarna began rehiring human agents. https://www.forbes.com/sites/quickerbettertech/2025/05/18/business-tech-news-klarna-reverses-on-ai-says-customers-like-talking-to-people/ (May 2025)
  • IKEA (Ingka). As its "Billie" bot took about 47% of call-centre queries, IKEA retrained about 8,500 call-centre staff as remote interior-design advisers, a channel reported at €1.3 billion in revenue (FY2022). https://fortune.com/2026/07/30/ikea-ai-workforce-reskilling-jobs-billie-chatbot-global-500/ (30 Jul 2026). Caveat: IKEA companies cut about 1,650 office roles in 2026, attributed to macroeconomic factors rather than AI. https://employerbranding.news/resources/what-ikeas-ai-reskilling-story-looks-like-now-it-has-made-layoffs-too/

Evidence: Strong on the long-run returns to social skills. The AI-specific evidence is emerging (job-posting data, surveys and case studies).

Tensions and risks:

  • "Human skills" can become a vague slogan. Schools need concrete, assessable versions of them.
  • Premium human service may widen inequality of access.

Implication for schools:

  • Graduates need deliberate practice with feedback in judgement (evaluating and deciding under uncertainty), collaboration, including with AI teammates, spoken communication, and ethical reasoning about AI use. These are not add-ons; they are the core of what junior roles now demand.

2. "Show the Board" shortlist

  1. The jagged frontier (Harvard/BCG). Consultants with AI were 40% better on the right tasks and worse than colleagues without AI on the wrong ones. Judgement about when to use AI is the skill. (2023, RCT)
  2. Novices gain most (customer support, QJE 2025). A 34% productivity gain for the least experienced agents: AI spreads expert practice.
  3. The skill-formation warning (Anthropic RCT, 2026). Juniors using AI to learn scored 17% lower, unless they used it to ask for explanations. This maps directly onto classroom practice.
  4. Doctors deskilled (Lancet, 2025). Detection without AI fell from 28% to 22% after routine AI use.
  5. The canaries (Stanford, Aug 2026). Young workers in AI-exposed jobs are 19% behind peers, through fewer hires. Set against IBM tripling entry-level hiring by redesigning junior roles around customer work and judgement.
  6. PwC 2026. Entry-level AI-exposed jobs are 7 times more likely to demand senior-level "human" skills.
  7. Organisation beats individual (Microsoft 2026). Culture and managers drive more than twice the AI impact of individual skill. This is a message for school leadership.
  8. Teachers save time without losing quality (EEF RCT). Planning time fell 31% with no quality loss. Yet only 18% of US teachers have formal AI guidance (Gallup 2026).
  9. Brazil and Estonia. MEC's nine free AI courses for teachers (2026), and Estonia's "teachers first, then students" rollout with a tutor that guides thinking.
  10. Canva and Zapier hiring. Candidates are expected to use AI in interviews and are scored on how well they direct and check it.

3. Signals of what's coming (2027 and beyond)

  • From assistants to agents, and humans as "orchestrators". Microsoft already frames the progression as Author, Editor, Director, Orchestrator (WTI 2026). Expect job and teaching roles to be defined by what people delegate to agents and supervise.
  • Measurement is getting harder. METR found developers refusing to work without AI, which makes clean control groups impossible. Expect less RCT evidence and more observational evidence. Productivity has turned positive in the 2026 data.
  • Entry-level redesign becomes a strategic choice. There is a split between firms that stop hiring juniors and firms, like IBM, that redesign junior roles, and apprenticeship-style "learn by supervising AI" models look likely to grow. Watch the Stanford "Canaries" dashboard: https://digitaleconomy.stanford.edu/project/indicators/canaries-dashboard/
  • Credentials for AI fluency (OpenAI Certifications, government badges, employer rubrics) will proliferate. Employers will start asking schools what "AI-fluent graduate" means in evidence.
  • Pedagogy-first AI tools. Examples are Estonia's "guides thinking" app and learning modes in AI products, with "learning interactions" making up about 30% of augmentation-style use in the Anthropic Economic Index. The differentiator will be tools and practices that protect skill formation.
  • Governance of teacher AI use. The policy gap on grading and feedback is the next pressure point, and more jurisdictions will issue formal guidance.
  • Human service as a premium (Klarna's "VIP" framing) and AI-skill wage premiums (PwC) point to a two-track labour market. That makes equity a strategic question for schools.

Synthesis:

  • When AI provides the effort, as in answer-giving, automation or unguided chat, practice performance rises and learning falls.

3. Critiques and risks

  1. Equity.
  • Gains depend on the conditions in which AI is deployed: devices, supervision, teacher capacity (World Bank Nigeria; Brookings 2026).
  • Tutor CoPilot helped students of weaker tutors most, which suggests AI can narrow gaps if aimed there.
  • Pew 2025 found higher ChatGPT schoolwork use among Black and Hispanic teens (31%) than White teens (22%). Who benefits depends on how students use it, not only on access.
  • Brazil's CPP has noted that enforcing the phone law has exposed inequality between schools. https://cpp.org.br/um-semestre-sem-celular-nas-escolas-aplicacao-da-lei-15-100-2025-escancara-desigualdade-educacional/
  • Strength: emerging.
  1. Data privacy and surveillance.
  • Human Rights Watch, "How Dare They Peep into My Private Life?" (25 May 2022), reviewed 163–164 edtech products endorsed by 49 governments. 89% surveilled children or could do so. https://www.hrw.org/report/2022/05/25/how-dare-they-peep-my-private-life/childrens-rights-violations-governments
  • In response: the UK DfE product expectations (2025), and Brazil's CNE ban on emotion recognition and social scoring (2026), under the LGPD and the ECA (Brazil's data-protection law and Statute of the Child and Adolescent).
  • Strong (documented).
  1. Teacher deskilling and loss of professional agency.
  • UNESCO's teacher framework and the OECD Outlook 2026 both warn against AI replacing teacher judgement. The CNE now bars AI from deciding grades or discipline.
  • The counter-evidence: Tutor CoPilot and the LearnLM/Eedi study found AI upskilled tutors' questioning.
  • An adjacent example from medicine is often cited: endoscopists detected fewer adenomas after regular AI use (Lancet Gastroenterology & Hepatology, 2025). It is [UNVERIFIED in this pass].
  • Strength: anticipatory for teaching.
  1. Homogenisation.
  • Doshi & Hauser, Science Advances (Jul 2024): AI ideas made individual stories rated more creative, especially for less creative writers, but stories became more similar to each other. https://www.science.org/doi/10.1126/sciadv.adn5290
  • Risk for schools: essays, project work and student "voice" converging on the same patterns.
  • Emerging.
  1. Cognitive offloading and assessment validity.
  • See 2b.
  • With 59% of US teens perceiving AI cheating, unsupervised take-home written tasks lose validity.
  • Brazil now bans AI essay grading and punishing students on the basis of AI detectors alone.
  • Strong for the offloading mechanism; emerging for long-term effects.
  1. Environmental cost.
  • IEA, "Energy and AI" (Apr 2025): data-centre electricity use was about 415 TWh in 2024 (about 1.5% of global use). It is projected to reach about 945 TWh by 2030, with AI the main driver.
  • Relevant to sustainability commitments common in international schools.
  • Strong (for the projection's basis). https://www.iea.org/reports/energy-and-ai/executive-summary
  1. Hype cycles and weak evidence.
  • The retracted Wang & Fan meta-analysis, and headlines such as "2 years of learning in 6 weeks", show that claims run ahead of evidence.
  • Kraft's scale-decay finding (0.44 SD falling to 0.16 SD) suggests pilot effect sizes for AI tutors will also shrink at scale.
  • Many studies are co-authored by vendors (for example LearnLM/Eedi).
  • Strong (a methodological point).

4. Recommendation: dimensions for a distinctive "Landscape of Great Schools"

How to be distinctive rather than a copy of OECD or UNESCO:

  • Describe observable school practice, what you would see on an ordinary Tuesday, not learner competency lists.
  • Give each dimension 3–4 maturity stages plus named "anti-patterns".
  • Attach an evidence rating to each practice, using EEF-style security ratings.
  • Cover K-12, higher education and professional learning with the same dimensions.
  • Build in cross-walks to external frameworks, so a school gets AILit, UNESCO, BNCC Computação and CNE/MEC compliance mapped for free.

Candidate dimensions:

  1. Learning design for cognitive ownership ("productive struggle by design").
  • Covers how AI use is designed so students do the thinking: hint-first rather than answer-giving; augmentation rather than automation; checks of unaided performance.
  • Rationale: the single strongest finding of 2024–26 (Bastani; Contractor & Reyes; Kestin; OECD "pedagogical intent"). No existing framework makes it a top-level dimension.
  1. An evidence-based core that AI amplifies.
  • Covers feedback, retrieval and spacing, metacognition, and tutoring dosage.
  • Asks whether AI is being used to deliver more of these with fidelity (as in Nigeria and Tutor CoPilot) or instead of them.
  • Rationale: keeps the framework grounded in strong non-AI evidence and guards against hype.
  1. Assessment integrity and redesign.
  • Covers valid evidence of learning when AI is everywhere: supervised and oral components, process portfolios, AI-use declarations, and no punishment based on detectors alone.
  • Rationale: existing frameworks largely skip assessment. Brazil's CNE now requires this, and it is where schools feel the most pain.
  1. Teacher expertise and professional agency.
  • Covers AI as a coach and a way to save time (EEF's 31% cut in planning time; Tutor CoPilot), and protected time for collaborative inquiry.
  • Teachers keep authority over judgements; AI never decides grades or discipline.
  • Rationale: turns the deskilling risk into an upskilling agenda, and aligns with UNESCO's teacher framework and the CNE.
  1. Belonging, relationships and wellbeing.
  • Covers human relationships as the non-negotiable core, context-sensitive belonging practices, SEL, phone and attention policy (Law 15.100), and monitoring of AI companionship and dependence.
  • Rationale: Walton 2023 shows belonging is causal and depends on context. Brookings flags risks to social and emotional development.
  1. Student agency and AI fluency: critical, creative, civic.
  • Covers students who can engage, create, manage and shape AI (AILit / PISA 2029), including resisting homogenisation (keeping an original voice) and judging the environmental and social costs.
  • Rationale: necessary for PISA alignment. We distinguish ourselves by stressing voice and originality (Doshi & Hauser) and continuity from K-12 through higher education to professional learning.
  1. Governance, safety and data stewardship.
  • Covers a risk-tiered inventory of tools (mirroring the MEC and CNE four-tier model), vendor standards (DfE-style), LGPD/ECA compliance, age-appropriate access (supervision in early years and Years 1–5), and transparency with families.
  • Rationale: legally binding in Brazil from 2026–27. Other frameworks treat this as a separate policy document rather than part of what makes a school great.
  1. Evidence culture and equity of impact.
  • Covers whether the school tests its own AI initiatives (small internal trials in the EEF style), disaggregates who benefits, and aims AI support at the students and teachers who gain most.
  • Rationale: addresses the hype cycle and the decay of effects at scale (Kraft). It turns "leading practice" from a list of tools into a learning organisation. Almost absent from OECD, UNESCO and ISTE.

For ISC Curitiba specifically:

  • Dimensions 3, 4 and 7 map directly onto the new CNE guidelines (approved 1 Sep 2026, with 12 months to comply).
  • Dimension 6 maps onto BNCC Computação (mandatory from 2026) and the IB/international expectations that come with AILit and PISA 2029.
  • That gives the framework an immediate compliance-plus-excellence value proposition.

2. What separates genuinely leading international schools from the rest

This is synthesis across the evidence above. Treat it as analytical judgement, not proven fact.

  1. They define learning first, then choose technology. WAB uses ACE principles and alumni profiles, Nord Anglia has Learner Ambitions, UWCSEA has Mission Competencies. AI policy follows from each school's definition of learning, not the other way round.
  2. Their AI rules differ by age and are published. UWCSEA shows each age band's rules. Brazil's new CNE rules require the same approach (see §3).
  3. They make evidence of learning visible beyond grades. Examples are New Metrics, MTC, and redesigned authentication (IB EE sign-off, process evidence).
  4. They invest in teachers before tools. Cognita and GEMS both pair platforms with staff professional learning and AI literacy. The leaders run pilots with evaluation before rolling out.
  5. They treat safeguarding and data as strategy, not IT. Examples are CIS safeguarding leads, UWCSEA's e-Safety Committee, and Brazil's LGPD/ECA Digital rules.
  6. They publish their policies. Public guides such as ISB Beijing's library guides and UWCSEA's technology page build family trust and hold the school to account.
  7. They use accreditation as their strategic engine. CIS "guided choice" and NEASC ACE reward schools that link accreditation to their strategic plan instead of running them in parallel.

3. Brazil / Latin America policy and market context

AI governance for schools (the most important new fact for ISC):

  • CNE national guidelines on AI in education, approved on 1 Sep 2026, apply to public and private institutions at every level. Provisions as reported:
  • Human oversight is required.
  • AI may not replace teachers.
  • No automated decisions on grades, promotion, retention, certification or discipline.
  • No autonomous or unsupervised student access to generative or conversational AI in early childhood and the early primary years; use must be planned and teacher-mediated.
  • No fully automated essay marking, and AI-detector results may not be the sole basis for punishment.
  • Schools must set internal data-governance rules.
  • 12 months to adapt.

Sources: Porvir https://porvir.org/cne-diretrizes-inteligencia-artificial-escola-universidade/ (Sep 2026); https://blog.fernandobarra.com.br/cne-regras-ia-escolas-universidades/. Data protection and child digital safety:

  • LGPD Art. 14 and ANPD Enunciado CD/ANPD 1/2023 (22 May 2023). Children's data may be processed under any legal basis in Arts. 7 and 11, as long as the child's best interest prevails. https://www.gov.br/anpd/pt-br/assuntos/noticias/anpd-divulga-enunciado-sobre-o-tratamento-de-dados-pessoais-de-criancas-e-adolescentes. Strong.
  • ECA Digital (Lei 15.211/2025). Signed 17 Sep 2025, in force 17 Mar 2026. It requires privacy-by-default for services aimed at or "likely to be accessed" by minors, reliable age verification (self-declaration not allowed), limits on profiling children for advertising, a loot-box ban, and parental linking for social media under 16. The ANPD enforces it, with fines up to 10% of Brazilian revenue, and ANPD implementing rules are still pending. https://www.planalto.gov.br/ccivil_03/_ato2023-2026/2025/lei/l15211.htm ; https://www.dataprivacybr.org/eca-digital-entra-em-vigor-o-que-a-lei-preve-e-o-que-ainda-falta-regulamentar/. Strong. The obligations fall mainly on providers, but they directly affect which edtech and AI vendors ISC can use.
  • Phone law (Lei 15.100/2025, 13 Jan 2025). It restricts personal electronic devices during class, recess and breaks in public and private basic-education schools. Exceptions cover teaching purposes, accessibility, health, and danger or force majeure. https://www.loc.gov/item/global-legal-monitor/2025-02-13/brazil-new-law-prohibits-cell-phone-use-in-schools/ ; https://www.cnn.com/2025/01/13/americas/brazil-restricts-smartphones-in-schools-intl-latam. Strong.

5. Considerations specific to Brazil for ISC

  • Compliance deadline. Plan AI policy, governance and data rules to meet the CNE 12-month window, counted from MEC ratification (to be confirmed). Watch for ratification.
  • Two regulators, one policy. Line the IB academic-integrity rules up with CNE's ban on AI detectors as sole evidence for sanctions, and with the ban on automated grading or promotion decisions. Build one ISC AI policy that meets IB, CIS, NEASC and Brazilian rules together.
  • Vendor due diligence under LGPD and ECA Digital. Keep an inventory of AI and edtech tools, set processing agreements, avoid sharing identifiable student data with external AI (a CNE recommendation as reported), and base decisions for under-18s on the child's best interest.
  • Early years. No autonomous generative AI for young children. ECC and early elementary use should be teacher-mediated only, consistent with ISC's Reggio Emilia approach.
  • Brazilian diploma track. Map BNCC Computação and the MEC stage-by-stage AI-literacy guidance into the Brazilian curriculum strand. Confirm Paraná state requirements.
  • Bilingual rules are unsettled. Watch for ratification of Parecer 2/2020 and any Paraná state council rule. These may affect staffing (teacher qualification) and time allocation.
  • Market position. In a fast-growing bilingual market, ISC's unusual status (IB, CIS and NEASC accreditation, American and Brazilian diplomas) is a differentiator. A principled, transparent approach to AI is a credible way to stand out against both bilingual franchises and the heavy AI use in Paraná's public system.

6. Signals for 2027 and beyond

  • IB digital exams expand beyond languages, with full digital delivery by the early 2030s (Emerging). The first new-model Extended Essays are assessed in May 2027 (Strong).
  • Competency credentials (New Metrics, MTC under ETS) may begin to be accepted by universities; results from the 2-year pilots are expected around 2027 (Emerging).
  • Brazil: CNE AI guidelines take effect for schools around late 2027, with a 24-month review of the guidelines and glossary. ANPD's ECA Digital rules on age verification and parental tools are due. PBIA indicators are tracked on OBIA/NIC.br (https://obia.nic.br/indicadores-pbia) (Emerging).
  • Networks are moving to AI agents and "teacher twins" (GEMS, targeted by the end of 2026). Expect parents to compare offerings and regulators to push back in Brazil's risk-tiered regime (Emerging).
  • Regional associations (Tri-Association 2026 programme) are shifting from AI tools to how schools stay human and how to "AI-proof" project-based learning (Anecdotal).
